Nobody has designed these stations: three TGV stations, at Paris Nord-Est, Gare de Lyon and Montparnasse, and a suburban station at Saint-Lazare. This chapter sketches where each would sit under the present station, what is already underground there, and how a passenger would get from the street down to the train. In the illustration used by the model, reaching a deep platform takes {{ m.extras.hall_deep | 1 }} minutes from the hall, against {{ m.extras.hall_today | 1 }} minutes to walk down a terminal platform today. That near-equality rests on assumptions that a concept design must check.

Paris Nord-Est: one station under two halls

How to read the drawings

Every drawing in this chapter is an illustrative sketch. Only dimensions with a source, or taken from the model, are printed as numbers. Positions, shapes and the order of the platforms are invented to make the sketch readable. Dashed outlines mark structures whose depth is not published.

Gare du Nord and Gare de l’Est are {{ d.stations.halls_apart | 0 }} m apart; the walk between them takes 8 to 14 minutes today.[[nordest]] Between the two, about {{ d.stations.magenta.depth | 0 }} m down, lies Magenta, a station opened in 1999 on line E of the RER, the express suburban lines that cross Paris underground: {{ d.stations.magenta.tracks | 0 }} tracks in a cavern {{ d.stations.magenta.length | 0 }} m long and {{ d.stations.magenta.width | 0 }} m wide.[[magenta]]

The new station would be entered from either hall. It has {{ m.cost.tracks['Paris Nord-Est'] | 0 }} platform tracks: {{ m.cost.detail['Paris Nord-Est'].hs | 0 }} for TGVs at {{ m.len.hs | 0 }} m, grouped three for trains towards the north and the east and three for trains towards the south, {{ m.cost.detail['Paris Nord-Est'].ter | 0 }} for regional express trains at {{ m.len.ter | 0 }} m, {{ m.sub_tracks['Paris Nord-Est'].n | 0 }} for RER G, the new suburban line that joins today’s lines P and N, and {{ m.sub_tracks['Paris Nord-Est'].h | 0 }} for RER F, which joins today’s lines H, K and L, both at {{ m.len.sub | 0 }} m. Each kind of train has its own platforms. Regional express trains run in the TGV tunnels, in the paths between the TGVs (see the hour); RER G and RER F each have a tunnel of their own. All the platforms sit side by side in one box, the {{ m.cost.detail['Paris Nord-Est'].ter | 0 }} regional express tracks on the west side. Northern regional trains leave the TGV tracks by plain turnouts; eastern ones, which run on to Gare de Lyon, cross the TGV lines by one dive-under at each end of the station assumption. Every train that uses Gare du Nord and Gare de l’Est today moves underground, so that all the surface tracks of both stations are freed: leaving even one line on the surface would keep most of the cost and lose the park. Once the surface tracks are cleared, the box can be dug in the open on the freed track field rather than mined under it assumption. The station is about {{ m.cost.platform_m['Paris Nord-Est'] / (d.stations.magenta.tracks * d.stations.magenta.length) | 0 }} times the platform length of Magenta. Nothing of this size has been built under Paris. The deep through stations of Stuttgart and Berlin have 8 tracks each (Berlin unverified).[[bt19, st_wiki_berlin]]

What is not known. Whether {{ m.cost.tracks['Paris Nord-Est'] | 0 }} tracks fit on one level between the two halls, at what depth, and how they pass Magenta, the RER station of Gare du Nord and three metro lines. This station alone is carried at €{{ m.station_total['Paris Nord-Est'] | 1 }} billion with design, land and 15% risk, {{ m.station_total['Paris Nord-Est'] / m.cost.total | pct }} of the whole project. A concept design of it is the first study to commission; see what to verify.

Gare de Lyon: beside a five-level RER station, near the Seine

Gare de Lyon has 22 surface tracks in two halls (unverified);[[st_wiki_lyon]] the seven platforms of Hall 1 are already 400 m long.[[st_etf]] Its underground station is one of the largest in Paris: RER lines A and D on five levels, 315 m long and 41 m wide, with RER D above RER A (unverified).[[st_wiki_rera]] The RER D tunnel is {{ d.stations.rerd_depth | 0 }} m deep.[[st_rerd]] Metro line 14 runs beside it (unverified).[[st_wiki_lyonmetro]]

The new station would have {{ m.cost.tracks['Gare de Lyon'] | 0 }} platform tracks of {{ m.len.hs | 0 }} m, {{ m.cost.tracks['Gare de Lyon'] / 2 | 0 }} each way. Three each way take the TGVs of the 12-minute beat. The fourth, one each way, takes the {{ m.ter_paths.loads.southeast.tgv | 0 }}th TGV of the hour on the south-east line, which six tracks could not hold, and the {{ m.ter_paths.loads.nelyon.ter | 0 }} regional express trains (TER) an hour each way that run through from Paris Nord-Est to Burgundy: it is busy {{ m.ter_paths.platforms.lyon_4th.used | 0 }} of {{ m.ter_paths.platforms.lyon_4th.avail | 0 }} minutes in the peak hour ({{ m.ter_paths.platforms.lyon_4th.share | pct }}). The TER share the TGV tunnel from Paris Nord-Est. All the TER of Gare de Lyon and of Bercy move underground and run through, so the TER tracks of Gare de Lyon and the Bercy station are freed. At its city end the TGV tracks fork: towards Paris Nord-Est, and towards the direct link to Montparnasse, {{ m.link_km | 0 }} km long, which passes under the Seine. The natural place for the station is under today's surface platforms, beside the RER station. Neither the station nor the junction has been studied; the geometry of the link is a study point.

Suburban line R stays on the surface, on a small terminal of a few surface tracks. It is the one open item left at Gare de Lyon. R cannot simply use the two new tracks: the fourth track each way is already busy {{ m.ter_paths.platforms.lyon_4th.used | 0 }} of {{ m.ter_paths.platforms.lyon_4th.avail | 0 }} minutes, and with the {{ m.ter_paths.r_option.trains | 0 }} trains an hour of line R it would need {{ m.ter_paths.r_option.lyon_4th | 0 }}, more than one track can give. A through train must also run on, and at Paris Nord-Est the regional express tracks would go from {{ m.ter_paths.platforms.nordest_ter.used | g }} to {{ m.ter_paths.r_option.nordest_ter | g }} of {{ m.ter_paths.platforms.nordest_ter.avail | 0 }} minutes. Running R through would therefore need {{ m.ter_paths.r_option.tracks_lyon | 0 }} more tracks at Gare de Lyon and {{ m.ter_paths.r_option.tracks_ne | 0 }} at Paris Nord-Est, {{ m.ter_paths.r_option.len | 0 }} m long, about €{{ m.ter_paths.r_option.cost | 1 }} billion with all mark-ups. The south-east tunnel itself could take R: {{ m.ter_paths.r_option.southeast_load | 0 }} trains an hour, {{ m.ter_paths.r_option.southeast_occ_15 | pct }} of its capacity with trains about 1.5 minutes apart, so no extra approach tunnel would be needed. Its partner beyond Paris Nord-Est would be extra trains on the branches of today's line P towards Meaux or Provins assumption. With no proven demand for those trains, this is left as a later option, for the growth that line R may see; it is not in the cost.

Montparnasse: under a station already covered by a slab

Montparnasse has 28 platform tracks, 24 in the main station and 4 at its Vaugirard annexe.[[ae14]] The platforms are one level above the street: Hall 1 is on level 2.[[st_gc_montp]] They are already covered by a slab that carries a garden of 3.5 hectares, the Jardin Atlantique (unverified).[[st_jardin]] Four metro lines meet around the station (unverified).[[st_wiki_montp]]

The new station would have {{ m.cost.tracks['Montparnasse'] | 0 }} platform tracks: {{ m.cost.detail['Montparnasse'].hs | 0 }} for TGVs, three each way, {{ m.cost.detail['Montparnasse'].ter | 0 }} for regional express trains at {{ m.len.ter | 0 }} m and {{ m.sub_tracks['Montparnasse'].n | 0 }} for RER G at {{ m.len.sub | 0 }} m. They arrive from Paris Nord-Est in two tunnels side by side, one for TGVs, which the regional express trains share in the paths between the TGVs, and one for RER G, and leave on the Atlantic side: the regional express trains towards Chartres, Le Mans and Granville, RER G towards Versailles and the south-west. At its city end the TGV tracks fork towards Paris Nord-Est, under the Seine, and towards the direct link to Gare de Lyon, which also passes under the river. This part of Paris stands over old limestone quarries: 770 hectares of the city are undermined, in eight arrondissements that include the 14th and 15th, where the station stands.[[quarries]]

Saint-Lazare: a suburban through station under a working station

RER F, which joins today's lines H, K and L, runs in its own tunnel from the north, under Paris Nord-Est and on to a new deep through station under Saint-Lazare, {{ m.corridor_km.nesl | 1 }} km from Nord-Est, a ride of {{ m.TR.SL | 0 }} minutes. Beyond it the tracks climb back to the surface in {{ m.corridor_km.slwest | 1 }} km and join the tracks of line L towards Pont-Cardinet, Clichy-Levallois, Courbevoie and La Défense, whose branches become further branches of RER F. The new station has {{ m.cost.tracks['Saint-Lazare'] | 0 }} suburban platform tracks of {{ m.len.sub | 0 }} m. It has no TGV or regional express platforms.

Saint-Lazare has 27 platform tracks today (unverified).[[st_wiki_sl]] The surface station stays: line J and the trains to Normandy keep their tracks, and the station keeps working during the works. Close by, about {{ d.stations.magenta.depth | 0 }} m down, lies Haussmann-Saint-Lazare, the twin of Magenta on RER line E: {{ d.stations.magenta.tracks | 0 }} tracks in a cavern {{ d.stations.magenta.length | 0 }} m long.[[magenta]] Metro lines 3, 12, 13 and 14 meet at Saint-Lazare (unverified).[[st_wiki_slmetro]]

Châtelet-Les Halles: a stop for RER G beside RER A, B and D

RER G stops once between Paris Nord-Est and Montparnasse, at Châtelet-Les Halles, where RER A, B and D and metro lines 1, 4, 7, 11 and 14 meet. Its tunnel passes under the Forum des Halles and widens there into {{ m.cost.tracks['Châtelet-Les Halles'] | 0 }} platform tracks of {{ m.len.sub | 0 }} m, one each way. A stop in passing needs fewer tracks than the ends of a line: RER A runs up to 30 trains an hour on one track each way at Auber and Châtelet, and RER G's {{ m.params.g_trains_h | 0 }} fit on one. The stop adds {{ m.G_STOP | 0 }} minutes, so Nord-Est to Montparnasse takes {{ m.TR_G | 0 }}. The platforms and the passages into the existing station come to €{{ (m.cost.station_cost['Châtelet-Les Halles'] + m.cost.items['Links into Châtelet-Les Halles']) * m.gross_up | 2 }} billion all-in assumption; where the platforms lie and how passengers reach RER A, B and D have not been designed. Nothing changes on the surface but new exits into the Forum des Halles.

Room for passengers: the halls once the trains are underground

Three of today's stations have a large covered hall at the street end: the historic halls of Gare du Nord and Gare de Lyon, and the covered platform level of Montparnasse. Each is about {{ d.stations.halls.hall_len | 0 }} m long. Passengers have about the first {{ d.stations.halls.wait_len | 0 }} m of it; the rest is taken by the ends of the tracks and their platforms (estimated from the SNCF station plans assumption). The waiting areas are crowded: Gare du Nord was designed for 500,000 passengers a day and takes more than 700,000;[[st_inquiry_nord]] at Montparnasse the concourse across the platforms is largely taken up by people waiting.[[st_apur_montp]]

With the platforms deep underground, the whole of each hall becomes a hall for passengers: a place to wait, meet, eat and shop above the platforms, and the way down to them. At Gare de Lyon this is Hall 1. The tracks beyond the end of each building become parks; at Gare de Lyon a few surface tracks stay for the small terminal of line R (see the parks where the tracks were). Gare de l'Est, the second entrance to Nord-Est, keeps its halls as an entrance and is not counted here; nor is Saint-Lazare, whose surface station stays in use.

How deep, and why

The new platforms have to pass under what is already there: metro lines, RER stations, sewers and the foundations of buildings. At Paris Nord-Est, Magenta is already {{ d.stations.magenta.depth | 0 }} m down. The model's illustration takes {{ d.stations.walk.depth | 0 }} m for all four stations assumption. That is within the range of stations that have been built, but it is not a design depth.

Depth does not strain the approaches. European rules allow tracks to slope by up to 35 mm per metre.[[tsi]] A tunnel can therefore gain or lose 35 m of depth in one kilometre (derived). Platforms 35 to 40 m deep therefore need only about 1.1 to 1.2 km of tunnel to reach the surface. The portals are {{ m.corridor_km.north | 1 }} to {{ m.corridor_km.southeast | 1 }} km from the stations, on the existing tracks at the ring road (at Rosa Parks for the eastern line), which leaves room for the climb beyond the junctions; the TGV stations are {{ m.corridor_km.nelyon | 0 }} to {{ m.corridor_km.nemont | 0 }} km from each other through Paris Nord-Est, and the direct link between Montparnasse and Gare de Lyon is {{ m.link_km | 0 }} km. RER F is the tight case: its tracks come back up {{ m.corridor_km.slwest | 1 }} km west of Saint-Lazare, room for a climb of about {{ m.corridor_km.slwest * 35 | 0 }} m at the steepest slope (derived). The link does not change the argument: even at a steady 35 m, it has room to dip under the Seine and come back.

The Seine is crossed in two places, between Paris Nord-Est and Montparnasse, where two tunnels pass under it side by side (TGV, which the regional express trains share, and RER G), and on the link between Montparnasse and Gare de Lyon, a TGV tunnel only. RER F stays on the right bank. Gare du Nord, Gare de l’Est and Gare de Lyon are all on the right bank; Montparnasse is on the left. Two lines already pass under the river in the centre: RER line B, on a ramp of 4%, and metro line 14 (unverified).[[st_wiki_rerb, st_wiki_m14]]

From the hall to the coach

Deep platforms are further from the street than surface ones. But a terminal has its own long walk: the train stands beyond the gates, and the average coach is {{ d.stations.walk.today_m | 0 }} m down the platform. A through station is entered from above, at several points along its length. Follow a passenger through both.

With platforms {{ d.stations.walk.depth | 0 }} m down, the deep station comes out at {{ m.extras.hall_deep | 1 }} minutes and the terminal at {{ m.extras.hall_today | 1 }} minutes. The difference, about {{ (m.extras.hall_deep - m.extras.hall_today) * 60 | 0 }} seconds, is well inside the uncertainty of such a sketch. The figures are the same at the three TGV stations: Gare de Lyon and Montparnasse have fewer tracks than Paris Nord-Est, which makes them narrower, not shorter, and a TGV platform is {{ m.len.hs | 0 }} m long at each. On a regional express platform of {{ m.len.ter | 0 }} m the average coach is about {{ m.len.ter / 8 | 0 }} m from a way down instead of {{ d.stations.walk.deep_m | 0 }}, and on a suburban platform of {{ m.len.sub | 0 }} m, at Saint-Lazare for instance, about {{ m.len.sub / 8 | 0 }} m (derived). No measured times are published for the Paris terminals or for deep stations. The State's regional travel model applies a penalty to deep stations; its value is not published.[[modus]]

What the model does with this

The central case takes the time inside the station as equal to today's. That is an assumption, and the result is sensitive to it: the sensitivity tests include two minutes more for every passenger, which removes {{ 1 - m.sens['Every passenger takes 2 minutes longer to board than today'].benefit_total / m.yearly.benefit_total | pct }} of the benefits.

Time inside the station is a separate matter from the time to reach its door. How long it takes to get from home or work to the entrance of a station where one's train stops is measured in reaching the station. The minutes on this page come on top, in both the present stations and the new ones.

How the station is run matters as much as how deep it is:

  • Today the platform is shown about 20 minutes before departure, and the gates close 2 minutes before.[[gates]] The passengers of a whole train, more than 1,000 when two sets are coupled, cross the hall in the same few minutes. SNCF tested showing platforms 30 minutes ahead in 2018.[[trial18]]
  • With fixed platforms, nobody waits for a screen. Passengers go down when they arrive and wait at the mark for their coach, as on Swiss platforms.[[sbb]]
  • Platforms by direction make the way down simpler. Every train for the south leaves from one of the same three platforms, so a passenger for Lyon or Marseille heads for the same part of the station every time, and the passage above the platforms needs fewer branches.
  • Two stops spread the crowd. The passengers of one train board at two stations, so each platform handles fewer people for each train.
  • The halls become waiting rooms. British design rules size a hall for the whole flow of the busiest quarter of an hour when the platform is announced late, and for a tenth of it when the platform is known in advance.[[nr]]
  • London trains board differently. Their passengers pass the border checks in a control hall of their own and board from a platform closed off for the time of the stop, as described for Paris Nord-Est.

A benefit that is not counted: for trains that run every two hours or less often, the official method adds a 20-minute margin before departure.[[fs25]] If fixed platforms let long-distance passengers arrive 5 minutes later, that would be worth about €{{ m.extras.margin_5min * 1000 | 0 }} million a year. No measurement exists of how early French passengers reach the station, so this stays out of the totals.
