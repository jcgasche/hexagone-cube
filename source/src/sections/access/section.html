    What “with the project” counts

    One station today, two with the project

    Today each direction has one terminal: Gare du Nord for the north, Gare de l’Est for the east, Gare de Lyon for the south-east, Montparnasse for the Atlantic. With the project every high-speed train stops at two of the three stations in Paris, no train changes direction, and a passenger boards at whichever stop suits best. Today’s station is always one of them. That is why the time can only get shorter.

    Which trains run where is set out in how the lines are paired and the direct link. The even split between the two other stations is an assumption, and so is free entry to Paris Nord-Est from both halls. The second one matters: with entry from the Gare du Nord side only, the Paris average at the station entrance would be {{ m.paris.nord_hall | 1 }} minutes and not {{ pts.avg.station | 1 }}, and people who live nearer to Gare de l’Est would be further from a train for the east than today.

    Two ways to count

    There are two honest ways to count the result. The map offers both.

    To the station entrance

    {{ pts.avg.station | 1 }} minutes on average, {{ pts.avg.today - pts.avg.station | 1 }} fewer than today.

    The minutes by public transport from a point in Paris to the entrance of the nearest station where the train stops. Where only half of the trains stop at the nearest station, the map takes the average of the two halves.

    This answers the plain question: how far am I from a station where my train stops? It is the measure used for the benefits of TGV passengers. On a trip of two or three hours, the few minutes the train then spends under Paris are spent seated on the right train with nothing more to carry or find; what matters to these passengers is a short trip to the station and no change on the way assumption. On this count {{ pts.share_quicker.station | pct }} of the points are quicker than today, by up to {{ pts.max_gain.station | 1 }} minutes.

    Counting the ride across Paris

    {{ pts.avg.ride | 1 }} minutes on average, {{ pts.avg.today - pts.avg.ride | 1 }} fewer than today.

    The same, plus the train’s ride between its Paris stops when you board at the first one: the {{ m.TH_ACCESS.L | 0 }}-minute run between any two of the three stations, by the link between Montparnasse and Gare de Lyon as through Nord-Est. The {{ m.stop_min | 0 }}-minute stop is not counted against the passenger, because the train is already at the platform when they reach it; the TGV measure of crossing Paris, for someone who stays on board, still counts it ({{ m.TH.L | 0 }} minutes).

    The passenger picks, among the trains for their direction, one that calls at the station that suits them best, as someone who cares about time would soon learn to do, and boards at the first stop only when that still puts them ahead. It assumes enough trains an hour to choose from without a longer wait assumption, which holds on the busy lines and less so on the east.

    This answers a stricter question: how much sooner am I on my way? It is shown for TGV passengers as a test, and it is the way regional and suburban passengers are counted, for whom a few minutes matter on a short trip. On this count {{ pts.share_quicker.ride | pct }} of the points are quicker, by up to {{ pts.max_gain.ride | 1 }} minutes, and the rest are unchanged.

    An example. Point {{ [r for r in pts.rows if r[0] == 275][0][0] | 0 }} on the map is in the 15th arrondissement, a few hundred metres from Montparnasse. Someone who lives there and travels to Lyon crosses Paris by metro to Gare de Lyon today: {{ [r for r in pts.rows if r[0] == 275][0][6] | 1 }} minutes. With the project half of the Lyon trains come from the Atlantic, stop at Montparnasse, {{ [r for r in pts.rows if r[0] == 275][0][7] | 1 }} minutes away, and run through the link to Gare de Lyon in {{ m.TH_ACCESS.LM | 0 }} minutes.

    The other half come down from Nord-Est, and for those Nord-Est itself, {{ [r for r in pts.rows if r[0] == 275][0][5] | 1 }} minutes away, is a little nearer than Gare de Lyon ({{ [r for r in pts.rows if r[0] == 275][0][6] | 1 }}). The first measure averages the two halves at the door: {{ [r for r in pts.rows if r[0] == 275][0][10] | 1 }} minutes. The second has the passenger always take a link train and adds the {{ m.TH_ACCESS.LM | 0 }}-minute run on the link, because until Gare de Lyon this passenger is no nearer to Lyon than someone who boards there: {{ [r for r in pts.rows if r[0] == 275][0][14] | 1 }} minutes.

    The minutes on the link are spent in a seat on the right train and not in the metro, which is worth something, but they are still minutes.

    Which measure the model uses

    The time saved by TGV passengers from the Paris region, and the extra TGV journeys it brings, come from the first measure, to the station entrance. The second measure is kept as a test in the sensitivity tests. Regional express and suburban passengers are counted the other way: their gain counts the train’s ride to the station that suits them, and assumes they pick a train that goes there, in regional trains.

    What the map leaves out

    The map does not include the time inside the station, from the entrance to the coach, which is a separate matter treated in boarding in a deep station; the model also tests what happens if every passenger needs two more minutes than today, in the sensitivity tests.

    How the 1,000 times were computed. A grid of 1,000 points, 315 m apart, covers Paris without its two woods. From each point the model finds the shortest time to the hall of each station on foot, by metro, RER and tram, with the average wait for each line, on the network of Île-de-France Mobilités as of 5 October 2026.[[metropolist]] Buses, taxis and crowding are not counted. The four directions are weighted by their share of high-speed traffic: Atlantic {{ pts.w.Montparnasse | pct }}, south-east {{ pts.w.Lyon | pct }}, north {{ pts.w.Nord | pct }}, east {{ pts.w.Est | pct }} (derived).[[art24]] The full method is in how access times are computed.

    Who gains, and what it is worth

    Every direction gains. At the station entrance the north and the Atlantic gain most ({{ m.by_dir.Nord.today - m.by_dir.Nord.station | 1 }} and {{ m.by_dir.Montparnasse.today - m.by_dir.Montparnasse.station | 1 }} minutes), the south-east least ({{ m.by_dir.Lyon.today - m.by_dir.Lyon.station | 1 }}): every train also stops at a second station, Nord-Est on the other side of the city or the other southern station through the link.     The numbers by arrondissement

    The suburbs

    The suburbs gain a little more than Paris, because their trains and RER lines arrive on one side of the city. At the station entrance the model finds {{ -m.access.central.clock.rings['Paris'] | 1 }} minutes saved for a trip that starts in Paris, {{ -m.access.central.clock.rings['Petite couronne'] | 1 }} in the inner suburbs and {{ -m.access.central.clock.rings['Grande couronne'] | 1 }} in the outer suburbs.     What quicker access is worth in the model

    The model counts the whole Paris region, at the station entrance, in “equivalent minutes”: the official French method counts a minute of walking or changing as two and a minute of waiting as one and a half, because passengers dislike them more than sitting in a train.[[dgitm]] On that scale the average trip to the train is {{ -m.access_effect.central.minutes | 1 }} minutes shorter.

    That is worth €{{ m.access_effect.central.benefit | m }} million a year in time saved and brings about {{ m.access_effect.central.journeys | 2 }} million more high-speed journeys a year before the charge on new uses (€{{ f.central.benefits['Time saved by TGV passengers from the Paris region'] | m }} million and {{ f.central.tgv.new_paris | 2 }} million with it, as the economic return counts them): British ticket data give about 11% more traffic for 10% less journey time.[[ww]]

    If the train’s {{ m.TH_ACCESS.L | 0 }}-minute run between its Paris stops were counted against the passenger, who then picks a train that calls at their best station, the trip would be {{ -m.access_effect.ride.minutes | 1 }} equivalent minutes shorter, worth €{{ m.access_effect.ride.benefit | m }} million a year and {{ m.access_effect.ride.journeys | 2 }} million journeys; the model keeps that as a test, not as the central case.

    Almost all of it comes from the choice of station. With the same three stations but each train boarded only at its own, the gain would be €{{ m.access_effect.no_choice.benefit | m }} million a year. The choice between two places to board is worth €{{ m.value_of_choice.eq | m }} million a year.

    These journeys are part of the high-speed traffic forecast, and the time saved is one line of the benefits.
