The project would add about {{ m.yearly.tgv_journeys | 1 }} million journeys a year on the TGV, the French high-speed train, and {{ m.yearly.reg_journeys | 0 }} million on suburban and regional trains. Most of the high-speed gain is between cities on two different lines: crossing Paris takes two or three changes of vehicle today, each of which turns passengers away, and a through train or a single change inside one station gives most of them back. The passenger charge falls only on these new uses, so no journey is lost against today: with it, {{ f.central.tgv.net_new | 1 }} million high-speed journeys a year are added.

What other cities saw

Many cities have joined suburban lines through their centre and counted the passengers afterwards. For long-distance trains the record is thin: no railway has published its passengers before and after a terminal became a through station.

  • The best cases gained 24% to 30% within one to three years. Stockholm's commuter trains gained 26% in two years while the rest of the network gained 3%.[[sl]] Zürich gained 24% in the first year of its 1990 through station, with twice the trains.[[sma]] Tokyo's Fukutoshin line gained 30% in six months.[[toyo13]]
  • Others gained little. Line E of the Paris RER, the express suburban lines that cross Paris underground, has gained about 10% so far on 30% more service.[[art25]] St. Gallen ran 25% more trains for 5% more travel.[[sg]] Philadelphia joined its two networks in 1984 during a collapse of service and saw no gain for ten years (unverified).[[philly]]
  • Where a whole new line came with it, traffic multiplied, mostly by moving passengers. London's Elizabeth line joined two suburban services through a new tunnel: 56 million journeys on the two in 2019/20, 220 million in 2023/24 and 242 million in 2025/26.[[til24, gla]] What made it different from a joined pair of terminal lines: new stations in the centre of the city, where the demand is, and far more trains, 24 an hour through the core. Only 9% to 10% of its journeys are new to public transport;[[tfleval]] in Sydney the figure is 27%.[[sydney]] The annex on the Elizabeth line has the detail.

Reading. A second stop in the city draws a real share of passengers, and removing a change of station is worth far more than the minutes it saves. But no case isolates the effect of a through station on long-distance traffic. That is why this study leans on British ticket data on changes, and on a model of time saved.

High-speed trains: two gains, both counted cautiously

Two groups of TGV passengers gain from the project, and each is estimated in its own way.

Starting or ending in the Paris region

{{ m.access_effect.central.idf | 0 }} million journeys a year today. With two stops in Paris for every train, the station where the train stops is nearer for most travellers: {{ -m.access_effect.central.minutes | 1 }} minutes nearer on average, with walking and waiting counted at their official weights, to the station entrance (see reaching the station).

The time model turns that into traffic: about 11% more for a journey 10% shorter.[[ww]] Result: {{ m.access_effect.central.journeys | 1 }} million more journeys, a gain of {{ m.access_effect.central.journeys / m.access_effect.central.idf | pct1 }}. If the train’s ride between the two Paris stops were counted against the passenger, who then picks the train that calls at their best station, the saving would be {{ -m.access_effect.ride.minutes | 1 }} minutes and the gain {{ m.access_effect.ride.journeys | 1 }} million; the central case does not count the ride, which a passenger on a long trip spends seated on the right train.

Crossing Paris

{{ m.conn.central | 1 }} million journeys a year today (derived from the 2012-13 station survey, the last count made; a fresh one is study point I12, see crossing Paris). Each of them changes vehicle two or three times in Paris, from the TGV to the metro or the RER, sometimes from one metro line to another, then to the next TGV; only between Gare du Nord and Gare de l'Est is it a single change, on foot. With the project {{ m.connecting.central.direct_share | pct }} of them stay on one train and the others change once, on the platforms of one station.

Result: {{ m.yearly.tgv_conn_journeys | 1 }} million more journeys, {{ m.connecting.central.uplift | pct }} more than today, before the charge. Trains from London, Brussels, Amsterdam and Cologne can run on to the south like any other, since their passengers are checked before departure; northbound checks are open (study point I5).

How the changes are counted. Each change of vehicle turns away part of the passengers who would otherwise make the trip: British ticket data find that an added change of train loses {{ m.params.uplift_direct | pct }} of them in the long run.[[ww]] This study applies that loss to each change of a Paris crossing today assumption, so that a trip with two changes keeps {{ m.params.change_keep ** 2 | pct }} of its potential traffic and a trip with three, such as Gare de Lyon to Montparnasse by metro lines 1 and 4, keeps {{ m.params.change_keep ** 3 | pct }}. The number of changes for each pair of stations is read from the metro and RER map, by the route with the fewest assumption. A direct train gives the whole potential back. A passenger who still changes does so between two trains inside one station. Trains are not timed to meet, as in the Swiss pulse: each direction's trains are spaced evenly over the hour, so the wait for the next one is short. Such a change is counted as half a change assumption, the rule used here for suburban trips across Paris, and keeps {{ m.params.station_keep | pct }} of the passengers. If it cost only what SBB, the Swiss railway, plans for a timed change, 12% less than a direct train,[[ch_sbbrules]] the gain would be {{ m.sens['A change inside one station costs 11%, as in a Swiss timed timetable'].tgv_journeys - m.yearly.tgv_access_journeys | 1 }} million journeys; if it cost a full change, {{ m.sens['A change inside one station costs as much as a change today'].tgv_journeys - m.yearly.tgv_access_journeys | 1 }} million. The shorter trip adds the time model on top: about 11% more travel for a trip 10% shorter, door to door.[[ww]]

What tempers it. The British loss was measured for one change of train inside a station, on routes of about 100 miles; applying it to every change of a Paris crossing is this study's reading, not a measurement. The one measurement made in Paris points lower: when the bypass line removed the change of station for trains between regions, their services carried {{ m.params.uplift_direct_high | pct }} more than they would have without it (derived), new stations included.[[bypass]] The low case therefore keeps the first estimate of this study, {{ m.params.uplift_direct | pct }} on direct trains and the time model for the others: {{ m.connecting.single.new | 1 }} million journeys. The high case puts every passenger of a served pair on a direct train: {{ m.connecting.high.new | 1 }} million.

The market the survey does not see. The {{ m.conn.central | 1 }} million are people who already cross Paris by train. The bypass line shows a wider market: its trains round Paris carried 21.9 million passengers in 2009, up from 10.2 million in 1999,[[interco_sud]] and they are slow for the pairs that include the Atlantic line, 4h47 from Bordeaux to Lille against 4h21 with a change in Paris[[bxlille]] and about 3h25 through Paris by this study's arithmetic (to verify) assumption. Passengers who go by car or by air to avoid the change are not counted either. None of this is in the estimate, which applies the growth only to people who cross Paris by train today: study point I13.

Capacity: what through platforms allow

A terminal platform holds a train for 30 to 40 minutes while it is emptied, cleaned and refilled, once the margin kept behind it is counted (derived from a 20-minute planning figure and a 26-minute quickest turn-round);[[rffcdg, ouigo]] a through platform holds it for {{ m.stop_min | 0 }}. Gare du Nord was turned down as the terminal of the airport express for lack of capacity.[[cdgx]] The new stations are sized for {{ m.extras.south_growth | pct }} more trains from the south than the standard hour assumed in this study (see how many platforms), and three tracks each way take {{ 3 * m.per_track | 0 }} trains an hour: all of the Atlantic line's {{ m.hour_full.Atlantic | 0 }}, and one fewer than the {{ m.hour_full.Southeast | 0 }} planned on the south-east line. Whether the platforms or the lines outside Paris cap the number of trains today is study point I1.

If the platforms are the cap, each 10% of extra high-speed traffic would be {{ m.extras.capacity_journeys | 0 }} million journeys a year, about {{ m.extras.capacity_journeys / m.yearly.tgv_journeys | 0 }} times the gain counted in this chapter, and about €{{ m.extras.capacity_revenue * b.track_share | m }} million a year of track charges for the railway. Whether it comes depends on the lines outside Paris and on demand, so it stays out of the central case; the sensitivity test with 10% more traffic is in what could change the answer.

The financing of the project asks something only of those who use what it adds. The passenger charge tested there is €{{ fp.charge_end | 2 }} for boarding or leaving at a station the passenger's line did not serve before, and €{{ fp.charge_through | 2 }} for crossing Paris, on a direct train or with a change inside one station. Each line still stops at its own station at today's price, so a passenger from the Paris region pays only by choosing the other station, when it saves more time than the charge is worth: {{ f.central.tgv.pay_share | pct }} of them would. What this means

New high-speed traffic comes mostly from the cities that Paris now separates. It adds to the case without replacing its base: the time saved by people who already travel, worth about €{{ m.yearly.tgv_benefit | m }} million a year to high-speed passengers and €{{ m.yearly.reg_benefit | m }} million to suburban and regional ones, the parks that replace the surface tracks and, outside this estimate, room for more trains later. The crossing estimate is also the least certain figure of this chapter: the economic return adds the benefits up, and what could change the answer tests each assumption, including the low case.
