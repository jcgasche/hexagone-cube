With trains to every part of France calling at two of the three stations, almost no one crossing Paris needs to change: only between the north and the east. So the timetable proposed here does not hold trains for connections. Every train stops {{ d.timetable.inputs.stop | 0 }} minutes at each station, and the trains of each direction are spread as evenly through the hour as the tracks allow, on a pattern that repeats every 20 minutes. A train to or from any line is then {{ d.timetable.cases.even20.paris_wait | 0 }} minutes away on average, a passenger on a direct train spends {{ d.timetable.cases.even20.pairs.AS.direct | 0 }} to {{ d.timetable.cases.even20.pairs.AN.direct | 0 }} minutes in Paris, and the change between the north and the east takes {{ d.timetable.cases.even20.ne_change | 0 }} minutes from train to train.

A sketch of a timetable, not a timetable

The minutes below come from a search over timetables that keep a few stated rules: platform tracks, the time a track needs between two trains, the spacing of trains in a tunnel, and the time to change. No signalling, junction or driver has been studied. No benefit of this pattern is counted in the rest of the report. The model is in model/timetable.py.

Why every 20 minutes

A regular timetable repeats. The question is how often, and the number of trains decides it. With the southern lines full, the service plan of this report runs {{ d.timetable.inputs.hour.AN | 0 }} trains an hour each way between the Atlantic line and the north or east, {{ d.timetable.inputs.hour.SN | 0 }} between the south-east line and the north or east, and {{ d.timetable.inputs.hour.AS | 0 }} between the Atlantic and the south-east by the direct link: see which trains pair. Every 20 minutes that is 2, 2 and 3 trains, whole numbers. A pattern of 10 minutes holds the standard busy hour ({{ m.hour_today.Atlantic | 0 }} trains an hour on each southern line) but not the full one; 15 and 30 minutes do not divide the {{ d.timetable.inputs.hour.AS | 0 }} link trains. So the full hour is the same 20 minutes three times, and the standard hour the same 10 minutes six times. The pattern is a template at full capacity: any path in it can be left empty when fewer trains are needed.

What "as evenly as the tracks allow" means. The search spreads, for each of the four lines, the trains leaving Paris to it and arriving from it; and, for each pair of lines, the direct trains between them. It also keeps the change between the north and the east short, and leaves gaps for regional express trains, which share the same tunnels: without that, trains every 4 minutes on a track that takes one every {{ d.timetable.inputs.headway | 0 }} would leave no room for them. Weights of these aims are a compromise found by trial assumption.

A passenger crossing Paris who picks a direct train waits, on average, for the next one between their two lines: about {{ d.timetable.cases.even20.direct_wait | 0 }} minutes from a random moment, weighted by the traffic of each pair of lines. The wait is spent at home or at the origin station, not in Paris, and it depends mostly on how many direct trains each pair has. This timetable, against two others with the same trains

Timed for changes: trains placed to shorten changes between services, as a Swiss pulse would. Untimed: the average of {{ d.timetable.cases.random20.n | 0 }} timetables drawn at random within the same rules.

Against a pulse timed for changes. A timetable can instead be built for changes, placing each train so that it meets as many others as possible. With every path a service to one destination, a passenger who changes then spends {{ d.timetable.cases.pulse20.change_avg | 0 }} minutes in Paris on average, against {{ d.timetable.cases.even20.change_avg | 0 }} with the even timetable. The price is a less regular hour: trains to the east, for instance, would come {{ d.timetable.cases.pulse20.even.line.E.out | 0 }} minutes apart on average from a random minute instead of {{ d.timetable.cases.even20.even.line.E.out | 0 }}. Since few passengers change, the even hour is preferred. The gain of timing is in any case limited by the tracks: with one tunnel track on each side of a station and trains at least {{ d.timetable.inputs.headway | 0 }} minutes apart, the five trains that a side of Montparnasse or Gare de Lyon takes every 20 minutes can never all stand together.

With the standard busy hour the pattern repeats every 10 minutes, one train on each of the six routes, and can be exactly regular: a train to the Atlantic or the south-east every 5 minutes, and the change between the north and the east takes {{ d.timetable.cases.even10.ne_change | 0 }} minutes.

Why trains are not held longer

A Swiss node makes connections both ways by holding every train for 6 to 9 minutes (see Zürich). In Paris a held train holds more people than it helps. For every passenger crossing Paris there are about {{ d.timetable.inputs.ratio | 0 }} who start or end their trip in Paris,[[art24]] and about half of them stay on board through one of the two stops, because their station is the train's second (derived from the 1,000 points of the access map). When the search for a timetable timed for changes was allowed to hold any train up to {{ d.timetable.inputs.extra_max | 0 }} more minutes at a stop, counting those passengers' time, it held {{ d.timetable.cases.pulse20.held_trains | 0 }} of the {{ d.timetable.cases.pulse20.trains | 0 }} trains of the hour.

What the timetable rests on

  • Spacing in the tunnels. {{ d.timetable.inputs.headway | 0 }} minutes between two trains on one track is about what the south-east line needs to carry the {{ m.hour_full.Southeast | 0 }} trains an hour planned for it,[[lgvse]] and more than the 2.5 minutes of the Elizabeth line's central tunnel at 24 trains an hour.[[el_may23]] With 4 minutes, no timetable of the full hour keeps every rule: the five trains of a side every 20 minutes would need the whole 20.
  • Regional express trains share the first pair of tunnels. In the gaps the timetable leaves, {{ d.timetable.spare['MP in'].spare | 0 }} more trains an hour fit on the Atlantic side of Montparnasse, {{ d.timetable.spare['LY in'].spare | 0 }} at Gare de Lyon and {{ d.timetable.spare['NE in'].spare | 0 }} at Nord-Est from the north and east, at the same spacing (model). With the pairing of this report, eastern regional express trains and half of the northern ones run on to Gare de Lyon. How many an hour they need is not yet modelled: study point I3 in what to verify. If they need more, the southern lines cannot all be full at the same time.
  • Trains on time. A late train delays the train behind it on a full line, and the change between the north and the east. Regular timetables rest on punctual trains: in Switzerland more than nine in ten arrive within three minutes;[[ch_sbbfacts]] see the Swiss timetable applied to Paris.
  • Destinations. The timetable fixes paths, not destinations: whether a path runs to Bordeaux or to Nantes, and how often each city is served, is a choice of service. The waits above are for the next train to a line, or between two lines.
  • Junctions. Trains crossing each other at the junctions north and south of Nord-Est and at the ends of the link are taken to do so on separate levels; the cost counts {{ m.cost.junctions | 0 }} junctions (see the prices used).
