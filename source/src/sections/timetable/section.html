The hour of high-speed trains is built here as one would with a table and a pencil. Every train stops {{ d.timetable.table.params.a_stop | 0 }} minutes and keeps its track {{ d.timetable.table.params.a_reserve | 0 }} minutes more for a late train; the track then stays empty {{ d.timetable.table.params.b | 0 }} minutes before the next train. So one track takes {{ d.timetable.table.params.per_track | 0 }} trains an hour. Today's busy hour needs {{ d.timetable.table.sides.today.MP['in'].tracks | 0 }} tracks each way at each station; with the southern lines full, {{ d.timetable.table.sides.full.MP['in'].tracks | 0 }} each way at Montparnasse and Gare de Lyon, and {{ d.timetable.table.sides.full.NE['in'].tracks | 0 }} and {{ d.timetable.table.sides.full.NE['out'].tracks | 0 }} at Nord-Est. Almost no one crossing Paris needs to change: only between the north and the east, {{ d.timetable.table.measures.full.ne_change | 0 }} minutes from train to train.

A sketch of a timetable, not a timetable

The minutes below follow a few stated rules: the time a train stops and holds its track, the time a track stays empty between two trains, and the spacing of trains in a tunnel. No signalling, junction or driver has been studied. The rest of the report still counts {{ m.stop_min | 0 }}-minute stops in its travel times and platform count; this annex uses {{ d.timetable.table.params.a_stop | 0 }}. No benefit of this timetable is counted. The model is model/table_timetable.py, which runs in seconds.

The track-by-track hour above keeps trains as regular as their tracks allow.

Why 7 minutes

At a Paris stop roughly half of a full high-speed train gets off or on, with luggage, through few doors: a double-deck TGV has far fewer doors for each passenger than a suburban train. The stops planned today at the busiest through stations give the measure (own count from the timetable data):

What the timetable rests on

  • Spacing in the tunnels. {{ d.timetable.table.params.headway | 0 }} minutes between two trains on one track is about what the south-east line needs to carry the {{ m.hour_full.Southeast | 0 }} trains an hour planned for it,[[lgvse]] and more than the 2.5 minutes of the Elizabeth line's central tunnel at 24 trains an hour.[[el_may23]] It also caps the tracks one side can feed in this way at four, as a fifth would start its hour too close to the first track's second train.
  • Regional express trains share the first pair of tunnels. In the gaps the timetable leaves with the southern lines full, {{ d.timetable.table.measures.full.spare_paths['MP in'] | 0 }} more trains an hour fit on the Atlantic side of Montparnasse, {{ d.timetable.table.measures.full.spare_paths['LY in'] | 0 }} at Gare de Lyon and {{ d.timetable.table.measures.full.spare_paths['NE in'] | 0 }} at Nord-Est from the north and east, at the same spacing (model), against today's peak of regional trains described in how many platforms. With the pairing of this report, eastern regional express trains and half of the northern ones run on to Gare de Lyon. This is study point I3 in what to verify.
  • Trains on time. A late train delays the train behind it on a full line, and the change between the north and the east. Regular timetables rest on punctual trains: in Switzerland more than nine in ten arrive within three minutes;[[ch_sbbfacts]] see the Swiss timetable applied to Paris.
  • Destinations. The timetable fixes paths, not destinations: whether a path runs to Bordeaux or to Nantes, and how often each city is served, is a choice of service. The waits above are for the next train to a line, or between two lines.
  • Junctions. Trains crossing each other at the junctions north and south of Nord-Est and at the ends of the link are taken to do so on separate levels; the cost counts {{ m.cost.junctions | 0 }} junctions (see the prices used).
