The stations would be built under five terminals that stay in use, and {{ m.cost.tunnel_km | 1 }} km of tunnel under the city. This chapter shows how such works have been done elsewhere, what would change at each of today's stations, and how the project could open in two stages. The model assumes {{ m.params.build_years | 0 }} years of works. That is an assumption, not a programme: there is no design and no ground investigation.

Four ways of building, all used before

Little can be dug from the street: the stations lie under working stations and the tunnels under buildings. Four methods have been used for works of this kind, in Paris or under a working terminal. Which one would serve where has not been studied.

1. A cavern dug from below

Workers reach the level of the station through a shaft, then dig the cavern from inside, a section at a time, and line it with concrete. Nothing is opened at the surface but the shafts.

  • Paris, Magenta and Haussmann-Saint-Lazare, 1999. Two stations of the RER, the express suburban lines that cross Paris underground. Each is a central vault with two side vaults, 54 m and 58 m wide for four tracks, about 30 m down.[[magenta]]
  • At Magenta the ground was first strengthened with 3,570 columns of injected cement, and it still gave way once during the works.[[magenta]]
  • The line ended 44% above its approved cost. The review names the complexity of the project and the hazards of underground work.[[cgpc]]

2. A box built from the top down, under working tracks

Walls are sunk from the surface and a roof is cast between them. Trains run again on the roof while the station is dug out underneath.

  • Zürich, Löwenstrasse, 2014. Four tracks 16 m under the terminal tracks,[[zvv]] built between September 2007 and June 2014.[[w_zh_start, zvv]]
  • Two or three tracks at a time, normally two, were shortened by 100 m; trains ran on all the others.[[w_zh_bartec, w_zh_tec21]] Part of the roof and walls had been built in advance, when the platforms above were rebuilt.[[w_zh_geo]]
  • Under a listed wing of the station, 110 m were dug beneath the standing building; it settled 10 to 15 mm, with 25 mm allowed.[[w_zh_fib]] See the Zürich annex.
  • Paris, Porte Maillot, 2024. Where nothing stands above, the box is simply dug open: 35 m deep, in 32 months.[[afgc]]

3. A station under a standing building

The building is first carried on new foundations. The station is then dug underneath it.

  • Paris, La Défense, 2024. The RER E station lies under an exhibition hall. Of the hall's 253 columns, 118 were moved onto about 1,800 small piles and a slab 3 m thick.[[d92cnit]]
  • The contract ran for 61 months.[[d92lots]] This station was under-estimated by €100 million, one of the named causes of the 45% overrun of the extension.[[d92over]]

4. Tunnels bored by machine

A cutting head as wide as the tunnel is lowered down a shaft. It digs forward and lines the tunnel with concrete rings behind it.

  • Paris, RER E extension, 2024. 8 km of tunnel 30 to 40 m deep, of which about 6 km in one tube 9.6 m wide inside for two tracks, with 10 shafts for air and rescue.[[eolekit]]
  • London, Elizabeth line. Eight machines bored 42 km of tunnel between May 2012 and May 2015.[[w_tunneltalk]]
  • Here: {{ m.cost.tunnel_km | 1 }} km of twin-track tunnel: {{ m.tunnels.km.tgv | 1 }} km for TGVs, the {{ m.cost.link_km | 0 }} km link between Montparnasse and Gare de Lyon included, which the regional express trains share, {{ m.tunnels.km.n | 1 }} km for RER G, the line joining today’s lines P and N, and {{ m.tunnels.km.h | 1 }} km for RER F, the line joining today’s lines H, K and L.

Method drawings illustrative sketch, not to scale.

The link and its two junctions. The link itself, {{ m.cost.link_km | 0 }} km under the city and the Seine, would most likely be bored by machine, like the other tunnels. Its two junction caverns, where the link leaves the corridor from Nord-Est at the city end of each southern station, are a different matter: a cavern widening under a working station, dug from below or from the station box, with the ground and the groundwater of each site unknown. Which method they would need is not known, and the geometry of the link is a study point. Each junction cavern is carried at €{{ m.rates.junction * 1000 | 0 }} million of works assumption. There are {{ m.cost.junctions | 0 }} in all, all on the TGV tunnels: these two, and the two on either side of Paris Nord-Est where the tunnels from the north and the east meet and where those to Gare de Lyon and Montparnasse part. The regional express trains have no tunnel and no junction of their own.

Two tunnels side by side. Between Paris Nord-Est and Montparnasse, {{ m.corridor_km.nemont | 0 }} km under the city and the Seine, the TGV tunnel, which the regional express trains share, and the RER G tunnel run side by side; the RER G tunnel widens into platforms under the Forum des Halles for its stop at Châtelet-Les Halles. Each would be bored as its own drive, from shared shafts and work sites, so that one corridor of work sites serves both assumption. The two cross under the Seine together; where, and at what depth, is not known. Elsewhere a corridor carries one or two tunnels: the north carries the TGV and RER F tunnels, the east the TGV and RER G tunnels.

Portals. The tunnels come up through {{ m.cost.portals | 0 }} portals onto existing tracks: {{ m.cost.portals_by_type.tgv | 0 }} for the TGV tunnels, {{ m.cost.portals_by_type.n | 0 }} for RER G (east and Atlantic) and {{ m.cost.portals_by_type.h | 0 }} for RER F (north, and west of Saint-Lazare towards Pont-Cardinet).

Ways in for the regional express trains. With no tunnel of their own, the regional express trains need links onto the TGV tracks: tie-ins at both ends of their platforms at Nord-Est and at Montparnasse, two dive-unders in the Nord-Est throats where the eastern trains cross under the TGV lines, and links at three TGV portal ramps, north, east and Atlantic. They are costed together at €{{ m.rates.ter_links | 2 }} billion of works assumption; their design, like that of the junctions, is a study point.

Saint-Lazare, under a working station. The RER F station is dug under a terminal that stays open throughout: Saint-Lazare keeps its surface station for line J and the trains to Normandy, during the works and after. Haussmann-Saint-Lazare, the RER E station close by, was dug in the 1990s as a cavern from below, the first method above.[[magenta]] How the new station would be dug, and how many platforms above could be spared in turn, has not been studied.

None of these has been done at this size

The Paris caverns have four tracks and the Zürich box four; the deep through stations of Stuttgart and Berlin have eight.[[bt19, st_wiki_berlin]] Paris Nord-Est would have {{ m.cost.tracks['Paris Nord-Est'] | 0 }}, under two terminals at once, Montparnasse {{ m.cost.tracks['Montparnasse'] | 0 }}, Gare de Lyon {{ m.cost.tracks['Gare de Lyon'] | 0 }} and Saint-Lazare {{ m.cost.tracks['Saint-Lazare'] | 0 }}, under a station that stays open. The price of that step is the largest unknown in the cost.

Whether to stop after the first stage cannot be judged yet, because its benefits on their own have not been estimated; see building in stages. The whole cost is built up in the cost chapter.

Twelve years, year by year

An assumed schedule

The model assumes {{ m.params.build_years | 0 }} years of works with even spending assumption. How those years divide between tasks and stages is an illustration made for this page. It is not a construction programme: there is no design, no ground investigation and no study of how many platforms each terminal can give up while it stays open.

Move through the years to see what is being built, what is open and what passengers find at each station.

Is twelve years plausible?

Smaller projects of the same kind took seven to thirteen years, and one is past twenty. Montparnasse ({{ m.cost.tracks['Montparnasse'] | 0 }} tracks), Gare de Lyon ({{ m.cost.tracks['Gare de Lyon'] | 0 }}) and Saint-Lazare ({{ m.cost.tracks['Saint-Lazare'] | 0 }}) are smaller than Paris Nord-Est and may take less time. The second stage holds most of the tunnel, {{ m.stages.second.km | 1 }} km of {{ m.cost.tunnel_km | 1 }}: boring it in about five years would need several machines at once, as on the Elizabeth line, where eight machines bored 42 km in three years.[[w_tunneltalk]] Whether that shortens or lengthens the critical path of the second stage is to be checked.

A longer schedule costs money as well as time, because interest runs during the works. Over {{ m.params.build_years | 0 }} years it adds {{ m.idc - 1 | pct }} to the cost. If the works took 15 years, the yearly cost of the project would rise by {{ m.sens['Works take 15 years, not 12'].cost / m.yearly.cost - 1 | pct1 }}; see the sensitivity tests.

Assumed here

  • {{ m.params.build_years | 0 }} years of works, with even spending.
  • The first stage opens after year 9, the second after year 12.
  • Every terminal stays open until its deep station is ready.
  • Each park opens some years after its surface tracks close, once the tracks are cleared: those of Gare du Nord, Gare de l’Est, Montparnasse, Gare de Lyon and Bercy after the second stage, when the last regional and suburban trains leave their surface platforms; part of the Gare de Lyon TGV tracks may be freed after the first.

Not known

  • The design of any station, and so the method and time needed to build it. The same for the link and its two junction caverns.
  • The ground, the groundwater and the old quarries at each site.
  • How many platforms each terminal can give up during the works.
  • Where the shafts and work sites would go, and how the spoil would leave. At Zürich, up to three trains of spoil left the site each day.[[w_zh_tec21]]
  • How long studies, public inquiry and approvals would take before the first year.
