A passenger who crosses Paris today spends about an hour between two trains, with a metro ride in the middle. With the stations joined, five of the six pairs of lines get direct trains and none of them reverses: {{ m.connecting.central.pairs['Montparnasse-Lyon'].direct | 0 }} minutes in Paris on a direct train for every pair, a {{ m.stop_min | 0 }}-minute stop at each of its two stations and a {{ m.run_min | 0 }}-minute run between them, by the direct link from Montparnasse to Gare de Lyon or through Nord-Est for the others, and a change of train happens on the platforms of one station. Suburban and regional passengers gain less on each trip, {{ m.regional.avg_clock_saving | 1 }} minutes on the clock on average, but there are {{ m.regional.users | 0 }} million such journeys a year.

TGV passengers who cross Paris

About {{ b.traffic.central.paris_base + b.traffic.central.cross_base | 0 }} million journeys a year by TGV, the French high-speed train, touch Paris: {{ b.traffic.central.paris_base | 0 }} million start or end there and {{ b.traffic.central.cross_base | 1 }} million cross it, changing between two Paris stations, usually by metro or by RER, the express suburban lines that cross Paris underground. Those who cross are counted at two stations, which is why the four terminals count {{ b.traffic.central.paris_base + 2 * b.traffic.central.cross_base | 1 }} million passengers.

Regional and suburban passengers

Today the suburban lines H and K (Gare du Nord), P (Gare de l’Est), N (Montparnasse), L (Saint-Lazare) and R (Gare de Lyon) and the regional express trains end at their terminals, unlike the RER. In the plan each suburban line that runs through has its own tunnel and the regional express trains share the TGV tunnels, so each train stops at a second station, and passengers stay on board when that station is nearer to where they are going.

Suburban passengers pay nothing more for it: the Navigo pass and the Île-de-France ticket already cover the metro ride they no longer make.

RER F

The suburban lines become two through lines of the RER, F and G. RER F joins today’s lines H, K and L: the trains of lines H and K run under Nord-Est to a new deep through station under Saint-Lazare, {{ m.TR.SL | 0 }} minutes on, stop included (the plan), and come back up on the tracks of line L, whose branches become further branches of RER F. Line L carries {{ m.params.lines.L * 1e6 / 260 | int }} trips a weekday; {{ m.params.through_trains.L | pct }} of its trains would run through the tunnel, the others ending at the surface station, which stays for line J and the Normandy trains, and {{ m.params.term_share.L | pct }} of its riders start or end their trip at Saint-Lazare assumption.

RER G and line R

RER G joins today’s lines P and N: line P’s trains run through Nord-Est and Châtelet-Les Halles to Montparnasse and on along line N, and line N’s trains run on to Nord-Est. At Châtelet-Les Halles its riders change to RER A, B and D and metro lines 1, 4, 7, 11 and 14. Line R stays on the surface at Gare de Lyon with no through running; its passengers see no change. Running it through to an eastern line is left as a later option.

What lies beyond the second terminal

The point of these lines is not the second terminal: P’s riders already have ways to reach Montparnasse, and RER E already serves Saint-Lazare. It is what lies beyond. Today’s line N serves the south-west’s clusters of research, universities and companies, Versailles, Vélizy and Saint-Quentin-en-Yvelines, with line 18 from Versailles to Saclay; joined to line P as RER G, it puts the homes of the north-east and the east much closer to those highly qualified jobs, an employment corridor.

What RER F gives

RER F gives the northern suburbs a ride without a change to Clichy-Levallois, Pont-Cardinet, Courbevoie and La Défense, large job centres, and relieves RER E, full in the morning. It also joins Saint-Lazare and the western suburbs directly to Gare du Nord, for the airport: line K, and an easy change to RER B towards Roissy, Villepinte and Tremblay-en-France.

Ride times assumed

Suburban trains are assumed to take {{ m.TR.L | 0 }} minutes between Nord-Est and Montparnasse, the same {{ m.run_min | 0 }}-minute run as the TGV and a stop of {{ m.TR.L - m.run_min | 0 }} minute; regional express trains, which stop {{ m.TER_STOP | 0 }} minutes, take {{ m.TR_TER.M | 0 }} assumption. RER G takes {{ m.TR_G | 0 }}, with its stop at Châtelet-Les Halles; the {{ m.G_STOP | 0 }} minutes the stop adds for riders who go through are counted against it in the economic return. The saving for each passenger is the difference between the trip from the old terminal to their destination in Paris and the trip from whichever of the two stops is nearer, measured on the public transport network as in reaching the station.

What the saving counts

It counts only the minutes gained to reach places in Paris near the second station. The employment corridors, the jobs and campuses beyond the stations, are counted apart in the economic return, on the census commuting flows and the region's timetable.

Why two minutes matter

  • Fewer people change onto the metro and the RER at the terminals. Gare du Nord and Gare de l’Est together see 800,000 travellers a day.[[nordest]] A commuter from the northern suburbs who works near Saint-Lazare or in La Défense no longer goes down to RER E at Magenta: the train goes there.
  • Crowding counts. In a survey of Île-de-France passengers, a minute in a full metro weighed up to 1.39 times a minute in an uncrowded one when seated, and 1.55 when standing.[[kroes]] The economic return values each crowded minute as on line 1 at the peak: €{{ d.french.study.central.benefits[d.french.names.key.crowd] | m }}m a year for the metro and RER legs no longer ridden, and €{{ d.french.study.central.benefits[d.french.names.key.g_crowd] | m }}m for line 4 and the other lines that RER G relieves (see the economic return).
  • Counted elsewhere: the employment corridors beyond the stations and the relief of the crowded metro and RER legs are valued in the economic return, on the census commuting flows and the region's timetable; more frequent trains if the lines are rebuilt as through lines are not. A passenger from Sarcelles who works in Courbevoie changes twice today: off line H at Gare du Nord, onto RER E to Saint-Lazare, and onto line L. Why such trips are rare

Such trips are rare today because they are nearly impossible, not for lack of demand. That is the reasoning behind line 15 of the Grand Paris Express, a ring from suburb to suburb. Each change costs passengers. British ticket data show that a journey with one more change of train loses 37% of its passengers;[[ww]] a trip that needs two changes keeps {{ (1 - m.params.change_loss) * (1 - m.params.change_loss) | pct }} of those it would have with none.

What the estimate removes

The estimate removes both changes for the pairs of lines joined by a direct train, and one and a half for the others, a change across one platform counting as half a change assumption.
