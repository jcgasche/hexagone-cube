Most of what the project returns never passes through a ticket gate: time saved by passengers of the TGV (the French high-speed train) and of regional trains, more comfortable journeys, a larger job market. Counted with the official French values, these benefits are worth €{{ f.central.benefit_total | 2 }} billion a year, {{ f.central.econ_cover_all | pct }} of the yearly cost of the triangle. Comparable tunnels were approved with benefits between half and twice their cost. As counted here, this project is {{ 'below that range' if f.central.econ_cover_all < 0.5 else 'at the low end of that range' | str }}, and this chapter shows what is missing and what is still to be counted, starting with the job corridors that the joined suburban lines N and H open: for regional trains the count is a floor.

What remains to the operators, once the extra trains are paid for, is not known and not counted.

Not yet counted, and study points

  • Trainsets saved. No train waits in a terminal, so the operators need {{ m.extras.fleet_sets_low | 0 }} to {{ m.extras.fleet_sets_high | 0 }} fewer TGV sets, about €{{ (m.extras.fleet_sets_low + m.extras.fleet_sets_high) / 2 * b.set_year | m }} million a year at €{{ b.set_year | m }} million a set. Half of it is counted as income in the operations account, none of it as a benefit here.
  • Punctuality. The Grand Paris appraisal counted it; its ratio would give about €42 million a year here (derived from that appraisal). It is left out because joined lines also share their delays (see the last row below).
  • London trains running on to the south. Arrivals from London are checked before departure and could run through; roughly €43 million a year assumption, see study point I5.
  • Passengers of the bypass line moving to through trains. Province to province passengers who go round Paris today would gain about an hour through it; roughly €105 million a year assumption, study point I13.
  • Growth. Benefits are held flat. At {{ fp.growth | pct1 }} a year they are worth {{ f.growth.grow | 2 }} times more over 60 years and cover {{ f.growth.econ_cover_all | pct }} of the yearly cost assumption, study point I17.
  • The job corridors of lines N and H. Line N (formerly P and N) puts the homes of the north-east and east within a direct ride of the research, university and business clusters of the south-west: Massy-Palaiseau, Saclay, Versailles, Saint-Quentin. Line H (formerly H, K and L) takes the northern suburbs without a change to Clichy-Levallois, Pont-Cardinet, Courbevoie and La Défense, relieves RER E, crowded in the morning, and joins the western suburbs to Gare du Nord and RER B towards Roissy. The present count stops at the Paris stations; this is a next step with its own method and better data.
  • The parks. Park areas on the terminal sites, not valued (I19, see below).

What public budgets get back

Added activity pays taxes, and part of the public money spent returns to public budgets. This can be estimated, in three layers that must be kept apart because they do not have the same standing. The answer depends above all on one question that this study cannot settle: how much of the activity added around the stations is new for the country, and how much is moved from elsewhere.

Tickets carry 10% VAT, which businesses cannot recover.[[eco_vat]] It applies to the ticket money of the new journeys: €{{ f.central.tax['VAT inside the new ticket money'] | m }} million a year in the central case. The charges themselves are taken as free of VAT. VAT lost on what passengers stop buying elsewhere is not deducted. Experience elsewhere is that this layer can be negative: the evaluation of High Speed 1 found a loss of tax of over £0.8 billion, because rail travel is taxed less than what it replaces.[[hs1]]
  • Layer 2: tax on added activity. France takes 43.6% of its output in taxes and social contributions.[[eco_insee]] The rate is not in doubt; what is in doubt is how much of a local gain is new for the country. British guidance starts from the assumption that none is, unless the project is shown to bring more people into work.[[eco_tag21, eco_tag23]] That is the first column. The middle column takes the wider effects as the appraisal of the Grand Paris Express counts them: each extra job is valued there at €10,000 a year of unemployment benefits saved,[[sgpi]] which is itself a public saving; output from denser job markets and from working time saved (for the {{ b.tax.business_pax | pct }} of TGV passengers who travel for work, unverified[[eco_biz]]) is taxed at 43.6%. The last column supposes that the {{ round(f.central.jobs, -2) | int }} jobs implied are all new and each pays tax on the average output of a job in the region, €123,220.[[eco_gdp]]
  • Layer 3: tax returned by the works. A simulation by the French economic observatory finds that 40% to 60% of debt-financed public investment returns to public budgets within five years, through wages, profits and lower benefits.[[eco_ofce]] This is real cash for the Treasury while the works last. It is not a gain from this project: any spending of the same size would return the same.
How official appraisal treats taxes

The French method does not count taxes as a benefit. It counts each euro of public money at €1.20, because raising taxes has a cost of its own, and it leaves out jobs created by the works and multiplier effects to avoid counting twice.[[eco_guide]] The builder of the Grand Paris Express claimed up to €4 billion of tax a year for output of €10 billion to €20 billion; the State's reviewers did not endorse a tax line.[[eco_senat44, sgpi]] For Crossrail, consultants put tax at 27% to 29% of added output.[[eco_volterra]] The table above is therefore a view for public finances, beside the appraisal and not inside it.

The reading. Between nothing and about €{{ round(f.tax_high.tax_total * 1000, -1) | int }} million a year comes back, €{{ round(f.central.tax_total * 1000, -1) | int }} million on the middle reading: {{ f.central.tax_share_of_annuity | pct }} of the yearly repayment of construction, {{ f.tax_high.tax_share_of_annuity | pct }} on the most generous reading. Tax receipts lower the net cost for public budgets; on no reading do they repay construction.

What the comparison says

  • On transport benefits alone, cross-city tunnels rarely return much more than their cost. Published ratios at approval run from about 0.5 to 2. Five of the thirteen stand below 1 on those benefits; they were built or are promoted for capacity, or rest on wider effects: Stockholm, Malmö, Gothenburg, Lucerne, the Grand Paris Express.
  • Wider effects decide the largest projects. They are a fifth to a third of the benefits in London, Sydney and Melbourne, and about 60% for the Grand Paris Express.
  • Measured returns are usually lower than forecast. High Speed 1 went from 1.5 to 0.64, Stockholm from 0.86 to 0.51, RER E from 21% to about 10%.
  • Per passenger, the cost is in the range of the others. About €{{ m.cost.total / (f.central.tgv.paris + f.central.tgv.cross + m.yearly.reg_users) * 1000 | 0 }} of capital for each yearly journey, against about €150 for the Elizabeth line and about €210 for Lucerne (derived). What differs is the starting point: Paris already has five RER lines through its centre, so the project does not open a crossing that did not exist, it joins the long-distance network to it.
  • This project is at the bottom of the range as counted here. On time and comfort alone, with benefits held flat, it is below all of them; with growth it is level with the lowest forecasts (Malmö, Gothenburg, Lucerne). Its counted benefits are those of a mid-sized scheme. Either the count misses its main purpose, capacity for more trains and a regional network that runs through, or the project is too large for what it delivers. Telling the two apart is the first job of a study.

What would have to be true

Could the benefits reach the cost? Not with any one favourable assumption on its own; only when several are combined. Growth and capacity overlap

Benefits can only grow {{ fp.growth | pct1 }} a year for 60 years if the trains can carry the passengers. The growth case therefore needs the capacity that the project is meant to unlock, and the two cannot be added to each other: the capacity line below is the condition of the growth line, not a further benefit on top of it.

Closing the rest would take benefits that are not counted here. Three could be large enough, and each can be checked:

  1. Capacity (study point I1). If the platforms are what stops more trains from running, the project should be credited with the traffic it unlocks. For scale: each 10% of extra TGV traffic is {{ m.extras.capacity_journeys | 0 }} million journeys and €{{ m.extras.capacity_revenue | m }} million of tickets a year, {{ m.extras.capacity_revenue / f.central.yearly_cost | pct }} of the yearly cost. Even counted in full, the gap of the central case would take about {{ (f.central.yearly_cost - f.central.benefit_total) / m.extras.capacity_revenue * 10 | 0 }}% more traffic, against {{ m.extras.south_growth | pct }} more trains planned on the southern lines. This needs the view of the track owner, SNCF Réseau, of when each terminal and each line is full, with and without the project.
  2. The regional network (I14). The count for suburban passengers rests on an average saving of {{ m.regional.avg_clock_saving | 1 }} minutes to Paris destinations and on ratios borrowed from another project; it is a floor. The job corridors of lines N and H, beyond the Paris stations, are not counted yet and need a method of their own. The region's travel model would show suburb to suburb trips, relief of the RER, frequency on joined lines and a real figure for wider effects.
  3. The parks (I19). About {{ m.params.park_ha | 0 }} hectares of tracks in the centre of Paris become parks assumption, the one at Gare de Lyon smaller or later while its regional tracks stay. Their value to the city is not counted and their cost is not in the construction cost; both need a decision and a valuation.

The tests that move the result most are in risks and next steps; the same levers can be moved by hand in the financing calculator.
