This chapter answers six questions in order: what the project costs to build, what it costs and earns each year, what it gives back to the country, what the through trains change across France, how the money and the benefits run year by year, and how it compares with other projects. Benefits are valued with the official French method for transport projects, the rules the State applies to every new line,[[fr_instruction]] and each rule is named where it is used. Two readings are given side by side. The study's simple reading holds the benefits of the opening year flat against the yearly bill. The official reading values every year from the first works to 2140 and brings it all to today's money.

How to read this. The project pays for its own running with a surplus, but not for its construction: public money carries {{ f.central.public_share | pct }} of the capital, as it does for every new line in France. The question of this chapter is whether what the country gets back is worth that money. On the study's simple reading, the benefits of a single year cover {{ d.french.study.central.cover | pct }} of the yearly bill. On the official reading, which lets traffic and the value of time grow over the life of the project, the country ends up €{{ d.french.cases.central.npv | 1 }}bn ahead in today's money.

The benefits, one family at a time

Nine families make up what the project gives back. Six come back every year and add up to €{{ d.french.study.central.benefit_total | 2 }}bn at today's traffic: passengers' time, the railway's own gains, comfort and ease, access to jobs, the environment, and the parks. Two happen once: the land and savings the project frees, and the carbon of the works, counted against it. One grows with traffic: room for more trains in the busy hours. The last, wider economic effects, is reported apart, as the French method requires. Each card gives the value, how it is obtained in two sentences, and the official rules applied.

Why the carbon figure stays modest

Air travel between the French regions is small: 8.2 million passengers in 2025, most of them to Nice and Toulouse, which stay beyond five hours by train even with the project, and the pairs the project puts under four hours carry a few hundred thousand each.[[dgac25]] The larger lever is the car, which carries most long-distance trips in France: a quarter of the new journeys counted in the model come from it, €{{ d.french.study.central.benefits[d.french.names.key.carbon_car] | m }}m of carbon a year in family 5, and the official scenario makes cars carbon-free by 2050, after which the saving is on accidents, noise and congestion only.[[fr_ref]]

What the table shows is the other half of the story: for weekends and holidays, millions of people in the regions gain a direct train every hour to regions they could only reach by changing in Paris. The count values that choice through the journeys it adds; it values nothing for the travellers who already had a direct train.

What the measurement says

  • The gain is in the north-west, not on the Saclay plateau. How the gain grows over the years

The count holds homes and jobs where the 2022 census found them. Firms and households move towards better access over fifteen to twenty years: the Grand Paris Express appraisal models that move and its reviewers note that the location effects are not complete at opening and keep rising to 2035.[[ce_l15]] Here the commuters' gain rises by half over twenty years in the central case, nothing in the low case, double in the high case, on top of the official growth of trips and of the value of time: €{{ d.french.timeline.jobs.users[12] | m }}m in {{ d.french.params.open | str }}, €{{ d.french.timeline.jobs.users[32] | m }}m in 2060, €{{ d.french.timeline.jobs.users[42] | m }}m in {{ d.french.params.last | str }}, then flat.

How the French rules were applied

The table says what each rule is, the value used here, and where on this page it applies. The study's own simple reading is kept beside it because it needs no forecast: it asks whether one year's benefits at today's traffic cover one year's bill.

How official appraisal treats taxes

The French method counts each euro of public money at €1.20 because raising taxes has a cost of its own, and it leaves out jobs created by the works and multiplier effects to avoid counting twice.[[eco_guide, fr_v5]] The builder of the Grand Paris Express claimed up to €4 billion of tax a year; the State's reviewers did not endorse a tax line.[[eco_senat44, sgpi]] For Crossrail, consultants put tax at 27% to 29% of added output.[[eco_volterra]]

The reading. Between nothing and about €{{ round(f.tax_high.tax_total * 1000, -1) | int }} million a year comes back, €{{ round(f.central.tax_total * 1000, -1) | int }} million on the middle reading: {{ f.central.tax_share_of_annuity | pct }} of the yearly repayment of construction, {{ f.tax_high.tax_share_of_annuity | pct }} on the most generous reading. Tax receipts lower the net cost for public budgets; on no reading do they repay construction.

Against comparable projects in France and abroad

The measure used everywhere is the ratio of benefits to costs over the life of the project, both brought to today's money. Above 1, the country gets back more than it spends. Under the French rules this project stands at {{ d.french.cases.central.ratio | 2 }} on the benefits counted and {{ d.french.cases.central.ratio_wider | 2 }} with the wider effects, directly comparable with the French appraisals of the Grand Paris Express and the Marseille tunnel, which use the same rate and horizon. The others are not strictly comparable, and the differences cut both ways:

  • French appraisals use {{ d.french.params.rate | pct1 }}, count effects to {{ d.french.params.resid_end | str }} and weigh public money at {{ d.french.params.cofp | 1 }}: the basis of this project's {{ d.french.cases.central.ratio | 2 }}. The study's simple reading, at {{ b.r | pct }} with benefits held flat, gives {{ d.french.study.central.cover | 2 }}, and {{ d.french.study.central.cover_wider | 2 }} with the wider effects.
  • British appraisals deduct ticket revenue from the cost, which raises the ratio. The Elizabeth line's 1.97 becomes about 0.8 to 1.2 on gross cost, derived from its published present values.[[eco_cr11]]
  • Australian appraisals use a 7% rate, which lowers theirs.[[eco_ia17]]

What the comparison says

  • On transport benefits alone, cross-city tunnels rarely return much more than their cost. Published ratios at approval run from about 0.5 to 2. Five of the thirteen stand below 1 on those benefits; they were built or are promoted for capacity, or rest on wider effects: Stockholm, Malmö, Gothenburg, Lucerne, the Grand Paris Express.
  • Wider effects decide the largest projects. They are a fifth to a third of the benefits in London, Sydney and Melbourne, and about 60% for the Grand Paris Express. Here they are {{ d.french.cases.central.pv_families['Wider effects, measured'] / (d.french.cases.central.pv_benefits + d.french.cases.central.pv_families['Wider effects, measured']) | pct }} of the benefits, because they are measured on this project's own flows rather than taken as a ratio.
  • Measured returns are usually lower than forecast. High Speed 1 went from 1.5 to 0.64, Stockholm from 0.86 to 0.51, RER E from 21% to about 10%.
  • Per passenger, the cost is in the range of the others. About €{{ m.cost.total / (f.central.tgv.paris + f.central.tgv.cross + m.yearly.reg_users) * 1000 | 0 }} of capital for each yearly journey, against about €150 for the Elizabeth line and about €210 for Lucerne (derived). Paris already has five RER lines through its centre, so the project does not open a crossing that did not exist: it joins the long-distance network to it.
  • Where this project stands. At {{ d.french.cases.central.ratio | 2 }} under the French rules it is level with Munich, Melbourne and Frankfurt at approval and above the Grand Paris Express on transport benefits; with wider effects, at {{ d.french.cases.central.ratio_wider | 2 }}, it is below the Grand Paris Express, Sydney and London, whose wider effects are larger ratios. Most of its benefits are measured on its own traffic; the least firm, the departure margin, the regular timetable and the room in the busy hours, are tested in the next section.

What could change the answer

Each bar changes one thing in the official reading and shows the net present value that results; the tick is the central case. Three questions matter more than all the others: the construction price, whether traffic grows, and whether the railway's own gains and the room for more trains are counted as the method says.

Growth and room overlap

Benefits can only grow {{ d.french.params.ld_growth | pct1 }} a year for thirty years if the trains can carry the passengers. The growth in the official reading therefore needs the room that the project is meant to unlock: the busy-hour room of family 8 is the condition of the growth, not a benefit on top of it, which is why it is valued only on the journeys the terminals would turn away.

What would settle the largest open points, and who can:

  1. Room for more trains (study point I1). The count values the journeys the terminals would turn away at €{{ d.french.params.cap_value | 0 }} each. The view of the track owner, SNCF Réseau, of when each terminal and each line is full, with and without the project, would replace that assumption. Without any value for room the net value is +€{{ d.french.cases.no_capacity.npv | 1 }}bn; at €{{ d.french.params.cap_value * 2 | 0 }} a journey it is +€{{ d.french.cases.capacity_40.npv | 1 }}bn.
  2. The regional network (I14). The job-access count is measured on the census and the timetable; a run of the region's own travel model would confirm it, show the relief of the RER and give a figure of its own for the wider effects.
  3. The parks and the land (I19). About {{ d.french.parks.central.ha | 0 }} hectares of parks and {{ d.french.parks.central.plots_ha | 0 }} of building plots are counted, on areas read from plans; a survey of the track fields would settle the hectares, and a decision of the City what is built and how many visits the parks can expect.
