Every long-distance train to Paris ends in a dead-end station. This study tests replacing the four terminals used by high-speed trains by three deep through stations, Paris Nord-Est, Gare de Lyon and Montparnasse, joined in a triangle of high-speed tunnels. Regional express trains share the TGV tunnels, in the paths between the TGVs; suburban line N runs in its own tunnel from the north and east through Nord-Est to Montparnasse, and suburban line H from Nord-Est to a new deep station under Saint-Lazare. No train reverses, every station has direct trains to every direction, and nobody is further from a train than today. It would cost about €{{ m.cost.total | 0 }} billion (€{{ m.cost_low | 0 }} to {{ m.cost_high | 0 }} billion across unit prices and risk), and on the benefits that can be counted so far it does not pay for itself.

The findings

The layout

Regional express trains in the TGV tunnels, a tunnel for each suburban line. TGVs keep their triangle: one tunnel on each side, {{ m.cost.tunnel_by_type.tgv | 1 }} km with the approaches. Regional express trains run in the same tunnels, in the paths between the TGVs, which needs signalling for trains about {{ m.ter_paths.signal_min[0] | 1 }} minutes apart: from the north through Nord-Est to Montparnasse and the Atlantic lines, from the east and from Gare de Lyon through Nord-Est to each other, so that none turns back. Suburban line N, formerly lines P and N ({{ m.cost.tunnel_by_type.n | 1 }} km), runs from the north and east through Nord-Est to Montparnasse. Suburban line H, joined by lines K and L ({{ m.cost.tunnel_by_type.h | 1 }} km), runs through Nord-Est to a new deep station under Saint-Lazare. The aim of the suburban lines is not the terminals but the jobs beyond them: line N joins the north-east and east to the research and business clusters of the south-west (Massy, Saclay, Versailles, Saint-Quentin), line H joins the northern suburbs without a change to Clichy-Levallois, Courbevoie and La Défense, and relieves RER E. Because every line at Gare du Nord and Gare de l'Est goes underground, their surface tracks are freed entirely for parks. At Gare de Lyon TGVs and regional express trains go underground, which frees the Bercy regional station too; line R keeps a few surface tracks. Financing

Operations pay for themselves; construction cannot be repaid. Track charges on the new journeys, charges on new uses of the TGV and on regional express passengers, and half of the trainset saving cover {{ f.central.ops_cover | pct }} of running costs, and the surplus goes to construction. No land is sold. Construction needs a grant of €{{ f.central.grant_bn | 0 }} billion, {{ f.central.grant_share | pct }} of the cost, on top of a business tax package: public money in all is about {{ f.central.public_share | pct }} of the cost.

The two accounts at a glance

What this means

As counted here, operations pay for themselves with a surplus, but construction is a public investment paid by taxes and grants for {{ f.central.public_share | pct }} of its cost: a grant of €{{ f.central.grant_bn | 0 }} billion on top of a new tax on businesses. Counted benefits return {{ f.central.econ_cover_all | pct }} of the yearly cost, at the low end of comparable tunnels. That is not yet a case for building, and not a case against studying: the count leaves out the reason most cities gave for through stations, capacity for more trains; the regional network run as through lines, and the employment corridors it opens from suburb to suburb, which the present measure does not count (it values only minutes saved to Paris destinations near the second station); the province to province market that would cross Paris without a change; and the parks on the freed terminal sites. It rests on prices and running costs taken from few records.

Six investigations would move the answer most: what limits the number of trains today; a fresh count of the passengers who cross Paris; the running costs net of what today's terminals cost; the region's travel model, for the regional lines; the value of the parks; and a method for the benefits of the suburban employment corridors. They are listed in what to verify. Gare de Lyon's regional express trains, Bercy's included, now run underground through to Nord-Est. Only line R stays on the surface: running it through to an eastern line would cost about €{{ m.ter_paths.r_option.cost | 1 }} billion more for {{ m.ter_paths.r_option.trains | 0 }} trains an hour with no proven partner, and is left as a later option.

How to use this document

The chapters follow the argument: the problem, what would be built, how it would run, what passengers gain, what it costs, who pays, what the country gets back, what could change the answer. Each can be read alone. Blue links with an arrow jump to the place where a point is treated; small numbers open the source.

Most figures can be explored: move a price in the cost chapter, a charge or a tax in the financing calculator, tap a point on the access map, pair two lines in crossing Paris, step through the works in the timeline.

Words used throughout. TGV: the French high-speed train. RER: the express suburban lines that already cross Paris underground. The link: the direct tunnel between the city ends of Montparnasse and Gare de Lyon, about {{ m.link_km | 0 }} km assumption, which lets a train from the Atlantic run on to the south-east without passing through Nord-Est.

Three annexes explain the precedents the project draws on: how Zürich turned a terminus into a through station under a working hall, what the Elizabeth line changed in London and how it was paid for, and how the Swiss timetable makes every train meet every train.

Every number comes from a small model described in method and assumptions, or from a document listed in the sources. Assumptions are marked assumption, drawings that are not designs illustrative sketch. No station has been designed; this is a first test of whether the idea is worth a study.
