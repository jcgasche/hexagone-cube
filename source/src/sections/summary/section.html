Every long-distance train to Paris ends in a dead-end station. This study tests replacing the four terminals used by high-speed trains by three deep through stations, Paris Nord-Est, Gare de Lyon and Montparnasse, joined by two pairs of tunnels and a direct link between the two southern stations, so that high-speed, regional and suburban trains run across the city. No train reverses, every station has direct trains to every direction, and nobody is further from a train than today. It would cost about €{{ m.cost.total | 0 }} billion (€{{ m.cost_low | 0 }} to {{ m.cost_high | 0 }} billion across unit prices and risk), and on the benefits that can be counted so far it does not pay for itself.

The two accounts at a glance

What this means

As counted here, operations can balance, with the charges on passengers and the income package, but construction is a public investment paid by taxes and grants for about its whole cost: a grant of €{{ f.central.grant_bn | 0 }} billion on top of a new tax on businesses. Counted benefits return {{ f.central.econ_cover_all | pct }} of the yearly cost, below comparable tunnels. That is not yet a case for building, and not a case against studying: the count leaves out the reason most cities gave for through stations, capacity for more trains; the regional network run as through lines; the province to province market that would cross Paris without a change; and the four parks on the terminal sites. It rests on prices and running costs taken from few records.

Five investigations would move the answer most: what limits the number of trains today; a fresh count of the passengers who cross Paris; the running costs net of what today's terminals cost; the region's travel model, for the regional lines; and the value of the parks. They are listed in what to verify.

How to use this document

The chapters follow the argument: the problem, what would be built, how it would run, what passengers gain, what it costs, who pays, what the country gets back, what could change the answer. Each can be read alone. Blue links with an arrow jump to the place where a point is treated; small numbers open the source.

Most figures can be explored: move a price in the cost chapter, a charge or a tax in the financing calculator, tap a point on the access map, pair two lines in crossing Paris, step through the works in the timeline.

Words used throughout. TGV: the French high-speed train. RER: the express suburban lines that already cross Paris underground. The link: the direct tunnel between the city ends of Montparnasse and Gare de Lyon, about {{ m.link_km | 0 }} km assumption, which lets a train from the Atlantic run on to the south-east without passing through Nord-Est.

Three annexes explain the precedents the project draws on: how Zürich turned a terminus into a through station under a working hall, what the Elizabeth line changed in London and how it was paid for, and how the Swiss timetable makes every train meet every train.

Every number comes from a small model described in method and assumptions, or from a document listed in the sources. Assumptions are marked assumption, drawings that are not designs illustrative sketch. No station has been designed; this is a first test of whether the idea is worth a study.
