In Switzerland the timetable was designed first and the works second. Trains from every direction reach the main stations just before the hour and leave just after, so that almost any change takes a few minutes. Lines were made faster only as far as needed to run from one such meeting to the next in under an hour.

The idea: all the trains are in the station together

A regular-interval timetable repeats itself every hour: each line leaves at the same minutes all day. The Swiss network has had one since 23 May 1982.[[ch_nc1982]] The pulse adds a second rule. At the main stations, called nodes, the trains of all lines arrive at about the same time and leave again together a few minutes later.[[ch_fc1994]] In the words of SBB, the Swiss federal railway, trains and buses reach a node just before the full and the half hour, or at minutes 15 and 45, and leave shortly after.[[ch_sbb1997]] The passenger does not hunt for a connection: it is standing at another platform.

      The nodes and the minute at which they beat

      The plan of 1994 named seven nodes. Zürich, Basel, Bern and Winterthur are served at the full hour and, for a large part, at the half hour. Lausanne, Biel and Luzern are served at minutes 15 and 45, because the runs to them are slightly too long for the hour. A node at 15 and 45 needs a train every half hour to give good connections.[[ch_fc1994]]

      The 2026 timetable still shows Zürich, Bern and Basel at 00 and 30, and Lausanne and Biel at 15 and 45. Luzern now gathers its trains around the hour, and St. Gallen behaves as a node of its own (derived from the timetable). Choose a station or a line.

      Why a run must take just under an hour

      If every train is in Zürich at the hour and every train is in Bern at the hour, a train between the two has just under an hour for the run. The Federal Council put it in one sentence: the concept requires journey times between the nodes of a little under one hour.[[ch_fc1994]] In general the run, with half the stop at each end, must fit a multiple of half the interval: 30, 60 or 90 minutes for an hourly service (unverified).[[ch_wikiitf]] Hence the Swiss motto of 1986: not as fast as technically possible, but as quickly as necessary.[[ch_cos1986]]

      The same thing on the real board. In 2026 the train from Zürich reaches Bern at :58. The trains to Basel and to Lausanne and Geneva leave at :04, the train to Visp and Brig at :07.[[ch_sch_zhbe, ch_board_bern]] With the 69 minutes of before 2004, it would arrive at :11, after all of them (illustration).

      Where the hour cannot be reached, the node moves. Bern to Lausanne takes 69 minutes, so Lausanne is a node at 15 and 45: the train leaves Bern at :34 and arrives at :43.[[ch_sch_bela, ch_fc1994]] Official maps show slots of 60 and 75 minutes on these western lines.[[ch_zeb2006]] A target of 57 minutes exists but has not been met,[[ch_fo365]] and the plan for 2035 assumes 68.[[ch_bav2024]] The western cantons speak of a two-tier network.[[ch_ctso]]

      What it took

      • A vote. On 6 December 1987, 57.0% of voters approved Rail 2000, on a turnout of 47.7%.[[ch_swissvotes]]
      • A budget that was cut to fit. The credit was CHF 5.4 billion at 1985 prices, about CHF 7.4 billion at 1993 prices. By 1991 the railway's plans would have cost about CHF 16 billion. The first stage was then capped at CHF 7.4 billion.[[ch_fc1994]] Of four new lines in the plan put to voters, one was kept.[[ch_huerlimann]]
      • One new line. Between Mattstetten and Rothrist, on the way from Bern to Olten: 45 km (47 km in two official papers), for CHF 1.5 billion in the press of 2002 and CHF 1,458 million in the budget of 1994.[[ch_swissinfo2002, ch_bav2006, ch_sbb1997, ch_fc1994]] It brought three runs to about one hour at once: Zürich to Bern, Bern to Basel and Bern to Luzern.
      • Trains instead of concrete elsewhere. On the line along the Jura from Lausanne to Zürich through Biel, tilting trains were bought instead of new line. Double-deck coaches gave 40% more seats without longer platforms.[[ch_fc1994]] Works were done only at bottlenecks or where a run between nodes took more than 60 minutes.[[ch_rg1997]]
      • The outturn. The first stage opened on 12 December 2004 and cost CHF 5.9 billion, CHF 1.5 billion under its ceiling.[[ch_bav2006, ch_swissinfo2002]]

      Three runs came to about one hour with the new line. Three others, in the west, are still 8 to 11 minutes over the hour, and all three are slower than planned in 1994.

      What it would mean for Paris

      An illustration, not a timetable

      This study has not drawn up a timetable. What follows shows what a pulse needs and what the through stations would make possible. No benefit of a pulse is counted in the model behind this report.

      A pulse needs one thing that Paris lacks: a place where the trains of all lines stand together. Today its main lines end in separate terminals, and a passenger between the north or east of France and the south-east or the Atlantic coast changes stations across the city. In the model this takes {{ m.connecting.central.pairs['Lyon-Nord'].old_clock | 1 }} minutes from train to train. The three through stations do not create one such place either. They are {{ m.TH.LM | 0 }} to {{ m.TH.M | 0 }} minutes apart by train, every high-speed train calls at two of them, and half of the trains of each line pass each station: in the standard hour, {{ m.service.flows['Atlantic to north'] + m.service.flows['Atlantic to east'] | 0 }} of the {{ m.service.hour.Atlantic | 0 }} Atlantic trains run through Nord-Est to the north or the east and {{ m.service.link_each_way | 0 }} take the direct link to Gare de Lyon and the south-east, and the south-east line splits the same way: see which trains pair. A timed meeting would therefore be partial at each station (derived from that pattern). At Nord-Est it could join the north and the east with the Atlantic coast and the south-east, and the north with the east, on the trains that pass there. At Gare de Lyon it could join the south-east with the north and the east, and by the link with the Atlantic coast. At Montparnasse it could join the Atlantic coast with the north, the east and the south-east. A passenger whose train does not call at the right station changes once more, or takes the next train of the hour. A change inside one station is taken as {{ m.connecting.central.pairs['Nord-Est'].change_clock | 1 }} clock minutes assumption, and about {{ m.connecting.central.direct_share | pct }} of the passengers crossing Paris would not change at all, because a train runs through: see crossing Paris.

      A pulse costs platforms. The number of platforms in this report assumes that stops are spread through the hour: {{ m.tracks_per_direction | 0 }} high-speed tracks each way at each station, {{ 2 * m.tracks_per_direction | 0 }} in all, each able to take about {{ m.per_track | 1 }} trains an hour by the rule used, and loaded at {{ m.service_full.nordest_load | 1 }} trains an hour per track at Nord-Est, {{ m.service_full.montparnasse_load | 1 }} at Montparnasse and {{ m.service_full.lyon_load | 1 }} at Gare de Lyon when the lines are full: see how many platforms. A timetable in which trains meet at fixed minutes would need more tracks, for two reasons. More trains stand in the station at the same moment, and each stands longer: in Zürich a through train that waits for connections stops for 6 to 9 minutes, against 3 minutes for one that does not wait (derived, see Zürich). The other side of the same coin: a pulse station is empty for most of the hour, so its average use, about 6 trains an hour per track at Zürich's Löwenstrasse (derived), is a design choice and not a ceiling. It says nothing about what six Paris tracks could take with stops spread evenly.

      What a pulse at the three stations would ask for

      • The same minutes every hour. The pattern tested in this report has {{ m.service.through_each_way | 0 }} high-speed trains an hour each way running through Nord-Est and {{ m.service.link_each_way | 0 }} on the direct link: see the hour. A pulse would fix the minutes at which they meet.
      • Three beats, not one. A train that meets others at Nord-Est on the hour reaches Gare de Lyon or Montparnasse {{ m.TH.L | 0 }} or {{ m.TH.M | 0 }} minutes later, so the southern stations would beat about a quarter of an hour after Nord-Est, and the link trains in between (illustration). Trains from the north and east in just before each beat, trains to the south out just after, and the reverse half an hour later.
      • Platforms known in advance. In Switzerland a line uses the same track hour after hour, and the track is printed weeks ahead (derived).[[ch_board_bern]] Platforms show where each coach stops.[[sbb]]
      • Trains on time. The Swiss pulse rests on more than nine trains in ten arriving within three minutes.[[ch_sbbfacts]]

      Where the Swiss example stops

      • The French timetable is not built this way. High-speed trains do not leave at the same minutes every hour on every line (unverified).
      • Seats are reserved. A Swiss passenger who misses a connection takes the next train half an hour later. A reserved seat is for one train, so a missed connection costs more and the margin must be wider. Today the platform is shown about 20 minutes before departure and access closes 2 minutes before.[[gates]]
      • Distances are longer. Swiss nodes are about an hour apart, so every train meets a node every hour. On French high-speed lines only the three Paris stations would be such meeting points, and no train calls at all three.
      • Few passengers change. In German long-distance travel 18% of trips include a change between long-distance trains.[[ch_weinhold]] In this study about {{ m.conn.central | 1 }} million journeys a year change between Paris stations today, {{ m.conn.central + m.connecting.central.new | 1 }} million with the new stations.
      • A pulse fills the platforms at the same minute. A station where all trains meet needs the most tracks of any pattern.[[ch_sma2022]] The stations of this report, with {{ 2 * m.tracks_per_direction | 0 }} high-speed tracks each, are not sized for it.
      The reading

      The through stations are not a pulse, and this report does not need one. They remove what makes a pulse impossible in Paris today: terminals with no train between them. They do not make one meeting point: with three stations {{ m.TH.LM | 0 }} to {{ m.TH.M | 0 }} minutes apart and every train calling at two of them, a pulse would be three partial meetings. Whether French high-speed trains should then meet at fixed minutes is a choice of timetable that can be made later. It would need regular trains that run on time, and more platforms than the six high-speed tracks a station counted here.
