The timetable of the plan runs regional express trains (TER) between the TGVs, two minutes from the train ahead and from the train behind, and in its fullest hour a minute and a half at {{ d.headway.cases.central_auto.hours.max.sum.close | 0 }} places. This annex checks those minutes against the trains themselves: their length, their braking, the slope down to the platforms, the points at the stations and the signalling. With the train performance the study assumes, two minutes hold on every track of the triangle and of the four approach tunnels, with {{ d.headway.cases.central_long.hours.full.sum.spare_min | 0 }} to {{ d.headway.cases.central_auto.hours.full.sum.spare_min | 0 }} seconds in hand on the closest pair of trains. A minute and a half does not hold when the second train is a TGV. The tight place is the arrival at a station down an approach tunnel, not the triangle. The result rests on the braking counted for a long TGV on the way down: with the least braking the European rule allows a high-speed train, two minutes still hold. A simulator lets every setting be moved.

What the timetable asks of the tunnels

  • Seven tunnels, two tracks each: {{ d.headway.cases.central_auto.hours.full.sum.tracks | 0 }} tunnel tracks. This annex is about the tunnels that TGVs and regional express trains share, and about the tracks the trains run on between stations. The three tunnels of the triangle and the four approach tunnels each have one track in each direction: 7 times 2 is {{ d.headway.cases.central_auto.hours.full.sum.tracks | 0 }}. The question is whether each of those times is long enough.

How the least time between two trains is worked out

A track is cut into stretches called blocks. A train may enter a block only when the train ahead has left it with its whole length, and a safety distance beyond. For the second train to run without slowing, it must be told that the block is free before the point where it would otherwise have to start braking.

So each block sets a least time between the two trains: from the moment the second train reaches its braking point to the moment the tail of the first has cleared the block, plus the seconds the signalling needs to see the block free, to move the points where there are any, and to tell the train. The largest of these times along the tunnel is the least time between the two trains. This is the method railway planners use.[[uic406]]

At steady speed, away from the stations
least time=braking distance + block + safety distance + train lengthspeed+signalling time+reaction time

At the stations nothing is steady, and the formula is not enough. The first train slows to cross the points, takes {{ d.headway.params.len.tgv / (d.headway.params.v_throat / 3.6) | 0 }} seconds to pull its {{ d.headway.params.len.tgv | 0 }} metres over each of them, then comes to a stand. The second is still at speed, and must be able to stop short of those points until they are free and set for its own platform track. The model therefore runs each train metre by metre, with its acceleration, its braking and the slope, and applies the rule block by block.

Two trains a few minutes apart never use the same platform track, since each stands there for several minutes. The model counts that they share the track as far as the last points of the fan, which is the least favourable case.

The hour is then replayed. Each pair of trains that follow each other on a track is given the least time it needs. The sum of these times over the hour is the share of the hour the track is taken. Planners keep that share under {{ d.headway.params.uic_peak | pct }} in the busiest hour on a line with mixed trains:[[uic406]] the rest is the reserve that absorbs small delays.

What the rules say about the braking of a TGV

The check above rests on the braking counted for a TGV. This part sets that figure against the published rules, and works out the arrival again with the least braking the rules allow a high-speed train to have.

  • The European rule for high-speed trains. On level track, a high-speed train must brake by at least {{ d.headway.rule.asked.service | 1 }} m/s² in normal service below 230 km/h, the brake being felt {{ d.headway.rule.asked.service_s | 0 }} seconds after it is asked. In an emergency, below 170 km/h, it must reach at least {{ d.headway.rule.asked.emergency | 1 }} m/s² with wet rails and part of its brakes out of use, after {{ d.headway.rule.asked.emergency_s | 0 }} seconds at most.[[tsi_hs_brake]]
  • The French rule for TGV lines takes up these values, sets them on level track, and limits the time the emergency brake takes to act to 3 seconds.[[epsf_f018]]
  • The times of the European signalling. In the worked examples of the EU Agency for Railways, the driver has {{ d.headway.rule.asked.driver_s | 0 }} seconds between the moment the cab tells him to brake and the speed he must not pass, and {{ d.headway.rule.asked.system_s | 0 }} seconds more before the system brakes by itself.[[era_curves]]
  • The slope. Going down the steepest approach, {{ d.headway.rule.slope * 100 | 1 }} in a hundred, gravity takes {{ d.headway.rule.drop | 2 }} m/s² from any braking.

What lines in service do

  • Switzerland, the new line from Mattstetten to Rothrist. Long-distance trains at 200 km/h under ETCS level 2, with times between trains of less than two minutes asked of the system.[[mr_etcs]]
  • London, the Thameslink tunnel. ETCS level 2 with automatic driving since 2018, to go from 16 to 24 trains an hour, one every two and a half minutes, with suburban trains.[[tl_ato]]
  • London, the Elizabeth line. 24 trains an hour in the central tunnel, also one every two and a half minutes.[[el_may23]]
  • The high-speed line from Paris to Lyon. The new signalling takes it from 13 to 16 trains an hour each way by 2030, one every 3 minutes 45 seconds.[[lgvse]]

Two minutes between long-distance trains are therefore at the edge of what is done, not beyond it. None of these lines brings 400 m trains to a stand in a station 35 m down at that rate, which is why the arrival is the point to study.

What this check leaves out

  • The stations are not drawn. The fan of points is a length and a speed, not a layout. Where the tracks of two tunnels meet on the same side of a station, a train of one may cross the path of a train of the other: that is not tested here (study point I2).
  • No train is late. The check says whether the timetable can run on time. What a delay does to the trains behind, and how fast the hour recovers, needs a simulation of the whole hour with real delays.
  • The stop at the platform is taken from the timetable. Whether a full TGV can be emptied and filled in the minutes given is a separate question (see the limits).
  • The braking is one figure for each train. A signalling design uses the braking curves of each kind of train, with their own safety margins, and the real slope metre by metre.
  • The lines beyond the tunnel mouths are not looked at. The study assumes they can carry the trains.
