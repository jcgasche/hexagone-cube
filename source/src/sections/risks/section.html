The case rests on a handful of things nobody has yet measured or drawn. Cost and the time needed to board in a deep station move the economic result most; the level of running costs decides whether the project can pay its own way once open. Most of these can be settled by studies that cost little next to the project, and twenty study points are listed at the end.

How sure is the result?

Each line changes one assumption and shows the share of the yearly cost that all the benefits counted then cover. The central case is {{ m.yearly.cover_all | pct }}. These tests are run without the passenger charges, which change the figures by less than one point. The one test that changes only the project's income, 10% more TGV traffic once the platforms no longer cap the lines, is left out of the chart and shown in the financing chapter: more trains would bring ticket money and track charges, but the benefit of the extra journeys is not valued here, which is why the case for capacity is still to be made (see what would have to be true).

  • Cost matters most. The same benefits cover {{ m.sens['High unit prices and 30% risk'].cover_all | pct }} to {{ m.sens['Low unit prices and 10% risk'].cover_all | pct }} of the cost depending on prices and risk.
  • Boarding time comes next. Two minutes more for every passenger removes {{ 1 - m.sens['Every passenger takes 2 minutes longer to board than today'].benefit_total / m.yearly.benefit_total | pct }} of the benefits: an extra {{ 0 - m.access.central.eq.all / 2 | 1 }} minutes on the clock is enough to cancel the whole gain in access. This is why the design of the Nord-Est station is the first thing to study (see inside the station).
  • How access is counted matters less. TGV passengers are counted at the station entrance. If the train’s ride between its Paris stops were counted against them, even with each passenger picking the train that calls at their best station, benefits would cover {{ m.sens['TGV passengers’ ride across Paris counted against them'].cover_all | pct }} of the cost assumption, study point I16.
  • Growth over time counts. Benefits are held flat in the central case. If they grow {{ fp.growth | pct1 }} a year, as values of time and traffic usually do, they are worth {{ f.growth.grow | 2 }} times more over 60 years assumption, and growth needs the capacity the project is meant to unlock.
  • The money side has its own tests. Running costs, the level of the charges and the response of passengers to them are tested in the operations account and can be moved in the calculator.

Stages and the decision

The project can be built in two stages: first Paris Nord-Est and Gare de Lyon with the north, east and south-east corridors and the tunnel between the two stations, {{ m.stages.first.km | 0 }} km of twin tunnel for €{{ m.stages.first.total | 1 }} billion all-in; then Montparnasse with its two corridors and the direct link to Gare de Lyon, {{ m.stages.second.km | 0 }} km for €{{ m.stages.second.total | 1 }} billion; see works and stages. The first stage serves the trains of three of the four high-speed lines and the suburban lines of the north, the east and the south-east. Its benefits on its own have not been estimated, so this study cannot say whether stopping after it would be a better or a worse project, and the staging itself is study point I8: the order, what each stage leaves usable, and whether the link can wait.

What to decide now

Not whether to build. The decision today is whether to spend on seven quick tests that can confirm or stop the case:

  1. Ask for ticket data on passengers who change station in Paris, by origin and destination (study point I12).
  2. Draw one standard hour of trains with the track owner (SNCF Réseau): every TGV stopping at two of the three stations, six tracks a station, no train reversing (I2, I3).
  3. Commission a concept design of the Nord-Est station at its smaller size, {{ m.cost.tracks['Paris Nord-Est'] | 0 }} tracks: position, depth, time from hall to train, and a first engineer's price (I2, I9).
  4. Ask what deep stations and tunnels cost to run, from the records of RER E and of the stations opened in 2024, net of what today's terminals cost (I11). Whether the project can pay its own running costs turns on it.
  5. Ask when each terminal and each line is full, with and without the project (I1). The economic case turns on it, and it is not valued in this study.
  6. Run the region's travel model on the two joined suburban networks: suburb to suburb trips, relief of the RER, frequency (I14).
  7. Value the parks: area, cost and worth to the city of the four terminal sites (I19).

With those in hand, settle the level and the legal form of the passenger charges (I12, I15), fix the stages (I8), and take the case to a full appraisal.

The case in three sentences
  • Operations balance, just. With €{{ fp.charge_end | 2 }} on each TGV passenger, the same on regional express passengers who use the second station, and half of the operators' saving on trainsets, income covers {{ f.central.ops_cover | pct }} of running costs ({{ f.high_running.ops_cover | pct }} to {{ f.low_running.ops_cover | pct }} across the running-cost range).
  • Construction needs public money for about its whole cost. The benefits counted are worth {{ f.central.econ_cover_all | pct }} of the yearly cost; a business tax package, a European grant and a grant of €{{ f.central.grant_bn | 0 }} billion add up to {{ f.central.public_share | pct }} of the €{{ m.cost.total | 1 }} billion.
  • The judgement rests on what is not counted: capacity for the next century's trains, a regional network that runs through Paris, and four parks where the terminals stood. Those are the benefits this study could not count, and the case stands or falls on them.
