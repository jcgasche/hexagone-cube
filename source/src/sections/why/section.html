Every long-distance line into Paris ends at a buffer stop, in one of four separate stations. So every train has to turn round, a passenger crossing France has to cross the city by metro, and the busiest stations are short of room for more trains. Other cities have removed the same problem by running trains through.

Four stations, four dead ends

High-speed trains (TGV) from the north arrive at Gare du Nord, from the east at Gare de l'Est, from the south-east at Gare de Lyon and from the Atlantic coast at Montparnasse. Suburban and regional trains end at the same four stations.

All four are terminals: the track stops at the end of the platform, and a train can only leave the way it came. A through station is the opposite: trains arrive at one end and leave by the other, without turning back.

Three things follow from the dead ends. A train holds its platform for a long time while it turns round. A traveller whose trip continues beyond Paris has to change station. And each line reaches only one corner of the city. The rest of this chapter takes them in turn.

Words used in this report
  • TGV: the French high-speed train. A full train is 400 m long; two coupled sets carry more than 1,000 passengers.
  • RER: the five express lines, A to E, that already cross Paris underground. Suburban lines H, K, P, N and R do not: they end at the terminals.
  • Regional express: trains from towns 50 to 250 km away (TER and Intercités), which also end at the terminals.
  • Hall: the large room of a station where passengers wait before going to the platforms.

A terminal uses its platforms badly, and the big ones are short of room

A train that arrives at a terminal is emptied, checked, often cleaned and restocked, filled again, and sent back out across the tracks it came in on. At Montparnasse the nine tracks kept for high-speed trains give at most nine departures an hour: one train per track per hour.[[ae14]] A TGV that only stops needs 3 minutes, or 4 to 5 at the largest stations.[[fnaut]] Through stations are planned for four to six long-distance trains an hour on each platform track: Stuttgart's new station, not yet open, reached about six in a simulated stress test.[[bt19, dhz]] One that is in service, Zürich's Löwenstrasse station, handles about 460 trains a day on four tracks, about six an hour on each on average,[[zvv]] though the through trains that wait for connections there stand 6 to 9 minutes (derived from the departure boards; see Zürich).[[zh_board_arr, zh_board_dep]]

Three tracks each way take {{ 3 * m.per_track | 0 }} trains an hour, about the most a high-speed line brings, 15 to 16. What limits the number of trains today, the platforms or the lines outside Paris, is a study point (I1 in what to verify). How the rule sizes the new stations is in how many platforms.

  • Gare du Nord was turned down as the terminal of the airport express for lack of capacity.[[cdgx]] It already handles 2,200 trains and 700,000 passengers a day.[[nordkit]]
  • Gare de Lyon must find room. The high-speed line to Lyon is being upgraded from 13 to 16 trains an hour each way by 2030, and the station has to take them.[[lgvse]]
  • The tracks cut the city. Approach tracks and platforms run from each terminal out to the ring road and divide the neighbourhoods on both sides. What the project would free is in changes to today's stations.

Crossing France means crossing Paris by metro

A traveller from Lille to Lyon gets off at Gare du Nord, takes the RER or the metro to Gare de Lyon and boards a second train. The last survey, in 2012-13, counted 13,000 passengers a day changing between two stations in the region.[[survey]] Scaled by the growth of high-speed traffic since, that is about {{ m.conn.central | 1 }} million a year (derived; no newer count is published). With the stations joined, the same traveller stays on the train, or changes on the platforms of one station.

Some trains already go round Paris. The bypass line east of the city, opened in 1994 to 1996, carries trains from one region to another without entering Paris, with stops at Marne-la-Vallée, Roissy and Massy. They carried 21.9 million passengers in 2009, 17.6 million of them on trains through those outer stations.[[interco_sud]] For the pairs that include the Atlantic line they are slow and few: Bordeaux to Lille takes 4h47 on the direct train round Paris and 4h21 with a change of station in Paris,[[bxlille]] against about 3h25 through Paris by this study's arithmetic (2h04 Bordeaux to Paris and 1h02 Paris to Lille on the best trains, about 20 minutes across the city; to verify) assumption. The passengers these trains do not carry, by car, by air or not travelling, are not in the count above; crossing Paris by TGV comes back to them.

The seats are short where the gain is largest. On the Atlantic line a new operator, Velvet, has ordered 12 trains to run between Paris and Bordeaux, Nantes, Rennes and Angers from 2028, and says it will add 10 million seats a year to a market where, by its own account, 15% of would-be rail passengers go by car, coach or air for want of trains.[[velvet]] The line to Lyon is being upgraded from 13 to 16 trains an hour each way by 2030.[[lgvse]] Demand is there; the terminals are where it meets its limit.

Two other groups of passengers pay for the dead ends.

  • Each traveller has one station to go to. A Parisian leaving for Bordeaux must reach Montparnasse, wherever in the city the trip starts. From the north-east of Paris that is a long metro ride with changes. Reaching the station measures it.
  • Suburban lines stop at the edge of the centre. Lines H, K, P, N and R each end at a terminal. A commuter from the northern suburbs who works in the south of Paris changes to the metro at Gare du Nord. Regional and suburban passengers gives the numbers.
Other cities have done it

Zürich put four through tracks under its terminal, Vienna replaced two terminals with one through station, and Stuttgart is replacing 16 terminal platforms with 8 through tracks.[[zvv, vienna, bt19]] Paris itself did it for part of its suburban network with the RER. The annexes on Zürich and London give the record of two of them, and the next chapter describes what would be built in Paris.
