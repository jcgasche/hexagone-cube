The same pattern of trains is assumed every hour, and no train reverses in Paris. A high-speed train runs from the north or the east to one of the two southern stations through Paris Nord-Est, or between the two southern stations by the direct link, so each train stops at two of the three stations. In a standard busy hour, {{ m.service.through_each_way | 0 }} trains each way run from the north or east to the south and {{ m.service.link_each_way | 0 }} each way run between Montparnasse and Gare de Lyon by the link. That pattern, with room kept for the day the southern lines are full, sets the number of platforms. Regional express trains run in the same tunnels, in the 2-minute paths between the TGVs.

One standard hour of high-speed trains

  • Every train stops twice in Paris, and never turns. A train from Bordeaux stops at Montparnasse, then either at Nord-Est, going on to Lille or Strasbourg, or at Gare de Lyon by the direct link, going on to Marseille. A train from Lille stops at Nord-Est, then at Gare de Lyon or at Montparnasse. Every station has direct trains to all four directions, on half of the trains of each line.
  • The same pattern every hour. The study assumes that the same trains run at the same minutes every hour. The timetable annex draws that hour on a beat of {{ m.stop_min + m.reserve_min + m.clear_min | 0 }} minutes: every train stops {{ m.stop_min | 0 }} minutes, every run between two of the stations takes {{ m.run_min | 0 }}, and trains from outside Paris reach all three stations at the same minutes, one every 4 minutes on each side. The travel times and the platform count of this report use the same figures.
  • Platforms are fixed. The 14:07 to Lyon leaves from the same platform every day, printed in the timetable, with the place of each coach marked on the platform.[[sbb]] What that changes for boarding is in the three stations.
  • High-speed trains are not for trips inside Paris. Tickets are sold from or to a station outside the region.

Any line can be paired with any other

The tunnels join all four lines, so a train arriving from one line can leave by any of the others: the northern and eastern lines meet the two southern ones at Nord-Est, and the two southern lines meet each other by the link. The timetable is free to pair them as traffic requires, and to change the pairing from one hour to the next. The choice holds at every station: from each of the three, there are direct trains in all four directions.

The four lines are not equal. The two southern lines carry more than twice the passengers of the northern and eastern ones: about 44 and 35 million a year counted at their Paris terminals, against 21.5 and 12 (derived).[[art24]] So not every train from the south can find a partner in the north or the east. In the standard hour, {{ m.service.flows['Atlantic to north'] | 0 }} trains from each southern line go on to the north and {{ m.service.flows['Atlantic to east'] | 0 }} to the east, which uses every train of the northern and eastern lines. The {{ m.service.link_each_way | 0 }} trains left on each southern line are paired with each other by the direct link, with no reversal. When the southern lines are full, {{ m.service_full.link_each_way | 0 }} an hour each way take the link and one south-east train an hour is left without a partner: it turns back at Nord-Est, the only train in the pattern that does.

For passengers, this gives direct trains on five of the six pairs of lines; only north to east has none, because the two lines meet just north of Nord-Est. Crossing Paris by TGV gives the times for each pair. Fixed pairs, with each southern line tied to one northern line, would be simpler to run but would leave only two pairs with direct trains; the model no longer tests that case, since the link makes it unnecessary.

How many platforms

The rule. Platforms are dedicated by direction: the tracks on one side of a station take trains heading south, those on the other side trains heading north, as at any through station. A stop takes {{ m.stop_min | 0 }} minutes; the track is then held {{ m.reserve_min | 0 }} minutes more for a late train, and after the departure it needs about {{ m.clear_min | 0 }} minutes before the next train can pull in. Each train therefore takes a place of {{ m.stop_min + m.reserve_min + m.clear_min | 0 }} minutes, and one track takes {{ m.per_track | 0 }} trains an hour assumption, all three figures, the beat of the timetable annex. Through stations elsewhere give the measure: Stuttgart's new station reached about six trains an hour on each track in a simulated stress test,[[bt19, dhz]] Zürich's Löwenstrasse handles about 460 trains a day on four tracks, about six an hour on each on average,[[zvv]] and Tokyo's Shinagawa was built with four tracks for 15 trains an hour each way.[[shinagawa_cap]] Through trains that wait for connections in Zürich stand 6 to 9 minutes (derived from the departure boards; see Zürich).[[zh_board_arr, zh_board_dep]]

Three tracks each way, and a fourth at Gare de Lyon. With the lines full, each station sees {{ m.service_full.stops_direction['Paris Nord-Est'] | 1 }} to {{ m.service_full.stops_direction['Gare de Lyon'] | 0 }} stops an hour in each direction. Three tracks each way take {{ 3 * m.per_track | 0 }}, one place every 4 minutes on a side, and the margin for late trains is inside each place, the {{ m.reserve_min | 0 }} minutes held after each departure. The Atlantic line's {{ m.hour_full.Atlantic | 0 }} trains an hour fit with nothing to spare; the {{ m.hour_full.Southeast | 0 }} planned on the south-east line by 2030 would not fit on three, by one train an hour. Shorter stops would free places: with {{ m.stop_min - 2 | 0 }}-minute stops a place would be {{ m.stop_min - 2 + m.reserve_min + m.clear_min | 0 }} minutes and a track would take 6 trains an hour, but passengers with luggage need time to board and alight a 400 m train, and Lyon Part-Dieu plans 6 minutes for most TGVs. So Gare de Lyon has a fourth track each way, {{ m.cost.detail['Gare de Lyon'].hs | 0 }} in all: it takes the {{ m.hour_full.Southeast | 0 }}th train of the hour and the regional express trains to Burgundy (see below).

The sliders change the rule, not the timetable: a longer stop, a longer clearing time or a lower share of the hour used all mean fewer trains on each track.

Regional express trains: in the TGV tunnels, on platforms of their own

Regional express trains (TER) have no tunnel of their own: they run in the TGV tunnels. The paths through the tunnels are laid on a grid of 2 minutes, {{ m.ter_paths.paths_per_hour | 0 }} an hour each way. The TGVs keep their places of the {{ m.stop_min + m.reserve_min + m.clear_min | 0 }}-minute beat, one every 4 minutes on each side, and the TER take the paths in between. Nothing changes for TGV passengers: the {{ m.reserve_min | 0 }} minutes held after each stop become the window between the public departure, when the doors close at the time printed in the timetable, and the railway departure, when the train leaves in its path. Every train, TGV or TER, takes the same {{ m.run_min | 0 }} minutes between two stations, so the paths never cross.

The condition is the signalling. Trains must be able to follow each other about {{ m.ter_paths.signal_min[0] | 1 }} minutes apart in the tunnels: European train control at level 2 (ETCS, the signals shown in the driver's cab) with short sections, and automatic driving in the tunnels assumption. Planners keep the busiest hour under {{ m.ter_paths.uic_peak | pct }} of what a line can carry, the usual European guidance (UIC leaflet 406, unverified). At {{ m.ter_paths.signal_min[0] | 1 }} minutes every tunnel stays under that line, the busiest at {{ m.ter_paths.max_occ_15 | pct }}; at {{ m.ter_paths.signal_min[1] | 0 }} minutes the Atlantic approach would reach {{ m.ter_paths.loads.atlantic.occ_20 | pct }}.

London trains and depots

  • London trains board from one of the six high-speed tracks at Nord-Est, closed off for each departure, with a larger control hall and waiting room above it (counted at €{{ m.rates.london_hall * 1000 | 0 }} million of works assumption). At about one departure an hour and 10 to 15 minutes of boarding, that fits within the rule above. Amsterdam already boards London trains this way, from one closed-off platform.[[amsterdam]] Trains arriving from London are checked before departure, so they can run on to the south like any other. Northbound, passengers boarding at a southern station for London would need to be checked before boarding: how, and where, is open (study point I5). Today's cross-Channel terminal at Gare du Nord is itself at its limits.[[an14775]]
  • Depots stay where they are; trains reach them through the tunnel portals (see changes on the surface).

Regional express and suburban trains through Paris

Each of the new tunnels joins lines from both sides of Paris. Regional express trains from the north run through Nord-Est to Montparnasse and on to the lines towards Chartres, Le Mans and Granville; those from the east run through Nord-Est to Gare de Lyon and on to Burgundy, and the other way. Line P from the east runs on as line N towards Versailles and Saint-Quentin-en-Yvelines; lines H and K from the north run on through Saint-Lazare onto the branches of line L. Line R does not run through. So three pairs of suburban lines have direct trains across Paris, H with L, K with L and P with N, and three more have one easy change at Nord-Est, between lines H or K and line N, and between line P and line L.

The point is less the second station than what lies beyond it. Line N joins the residential north-east and east to the research, university and company clusters of the south-west, around Versailles, Saint-Quentin-en-Yvelines and the Saclay plateau. Line H gives the northern suburbs a ride without a change to Clichy-Levallois, Pont-Cardinet, Courbevoie and La Défense, relieving RER line E, full in the morning, and joins Saint-Lazare and the western suburbs directly to Gare du Nord and its trains to the airport. Few people cross Paris from suburb to suburb today because it is so hard, not for lack of reasons to: the Grand Paris Express line 15 was built on that idea. What the through trains are worth to their passengers, and what that measure does not yet count, is in regional and suburban passengers.
