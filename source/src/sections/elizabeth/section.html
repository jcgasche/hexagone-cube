London's Elizabeth line is the closest recent case of joining two sides of a capital by a deep tunnel with large stations. It opened in 2022, three and a half years late and 28% over budget. It now carries more than 700,000 trips a day and its fares cover its running cost, yet most of its passengers were already on rail and save a few minutes each. It is also far smaller and simpler than what Paris would need.

The line and its stations

Stopping trains from the west used to end at Paddington station, and stopping trains from the east at Liverpool Street station, two terminals at the edge of the centre.[[el_map22]] The project, called Crossrail while it was built, joined them with 21 km of new twin tunnel under central London, one tube for each direction, and added a branch to the south-east.[[el_asce]] The result is one line of more than 100 km and 41 stations, 10 of them new.[[el_framework]] It is run by Transport for London (TfL), the city's transport authority.

Journey times before and after

Paddington to Liverpool Street takes 11 minutes instead of about 20.[[el_ianvisits]] The largest gains are on the new south-east branch, which had no direct railway to the centre. On the older branches the saving is smaller, but the change of train at the terminal disappears. Where the Underground already ran directly, the line saves nothing.

What the official evaluation measured

TfL and the national transport ministry published an evaluation of the first two years in 2025.[[el_evalfull]] It counts perceived minutes: minutes weighted for waiting, crowding and delay, as a passenger feels them.

  • Small savings for many people. 95% of the minutes saved go to trips that were already being made (derived), including on other lines that became less crowded.
  • Measured from door to door, the typical peak journey between the Paddington area and the Abbey Wood area fell from 74.5 to 45 minutes between 2019 and 2023.[[el_evalfull]]
  • Along the older branches many routes show no significant fall, since trains already ran there. The report gives no minutes for the change of train that disappeared.[[el_evalfull]]
  • The size of the saving that carried London's case is 1.3 perceived minutes a trip across all trips, 6.3 for the new ones;[[el_evalfull]] this study counts about {{ m.regional.avg_clock_saving | 1 }} clock minutes a journey for the regional passengers who gain a second stop in Paris, before any weighting: see regional traffic.

How traffic grew, and where it came from

The line opened in three steps over twelve months, and traffic rose at each one. Against the last year before the pandemic, yearly journeys have multiplied by 4.3: from 56 million on the two older services to 242 million in 2025/26 (derived).[[til24, gla]]

Where the passengers came from

  • New trips are about a tenth. The evaluation finds 71,000 more public transport trips a weekday across London, about 1% of the city's total and a tenth of the line's own (derived).[[el_evalfull]] TfL's first estimate, a year after opening, was twice that (unverified).[[el_ianv23]]
  • The older services kept their traffic; the growth is elsewhere. The traffic that came from them equals about 58 million journeys a year, almost what they carried in 2019/20 (derived).[[el_til23, til24]]
  • Other lines were relieved. Parallel rail links carry 16% to 22% less than comparable links (derived).[[el_evalfull]] The line itself has room: in January 2024 it was never above 3 standing passengers per square metre.[[el_evalfull]]

What multiplied the traffic, and what Paris would have of it

Three things multiplied the journeys by 4.3. Six new stations were opened in the centre, on a route that had no railway, each spanning two older stations and joined to the Underground. Trains run through instead of ending at Paddington and Liverpool Street, so the change of train at the terminal disappeared. And there are far more trains: 24 an hour through the centre against 15 or 16 into the Liverpool Street terminal before, plus a new branch to the south-east.[[el_journey, el_ops]] The three stations of this study give Paris the second of these, and part of the first: the second stop in the centre is a new station for the passenger who used it, though not on a route without rail. They do not give the third. The model keeps today's trains on the suburban lines and adds regional express trains only; it counts the time saved by the second stop and the journeys that follow from it, at {{ m.regional.new | 1 }} million a year, and nothing for more trains, less crowding or journeys across the region. Those are left to study point I14: see what must be verified.

What this says for a forecast

Most users of a new through line are people already on rail who save a few minutes. New trips are about a tenth, and the shift from cars is close to nothing. Demand rose 6% to 9% where journeys became 10% shorter.[[el_til23]] This study builds its regional forecast the same way, with an average saving of {{ m.regional.avg_clock_saving | 1 }} minutes a journey: see regional traffic and the evidence.

In 2026 euros the final cost is about €32 billion, an order of magnitude converted for this study with British consumer prices and the exchange rate of October 2026.[[nao21, el_ecb, el_ukcpi]] The tunnels were finished in 2015.[[el_tunneltalk15]] The time was lost afterwards, in the stations and in making trains, signalling and station equipment work together; the delay became apparent only in late 2018.[[el_nao19s]] Of the last £1.9 billion of cost increase, £0.9 billion was the cost of the delay itself.[[el_pac21]]

Who paid

The supplement on business rates. Business rates are the British tax on business property, charged on the rental value of the premises. Since 2010 London adds 2 pence per pound of that value, the legal maximum, on its larger premises, 13% of them.[[el_md3465]] The supplement was to pay £4.1 billion, 28% of the budget of 2010 (derived).[[el_nao14]]

The overrun was covered by government loans, most of them to the Greater London Authority, the city's government.[[el_pac21]] With a levy on new floorspace paid by developers, the Authority's contribution reached £6.9 billion, 37% of the final funding of £18.8 billion (derived).[[el_md3465, nao21]] It borrowed the money and repays it from the two levies. The supplement is due to end between 2038 and 2041.[[el_md3465]]

Fares and running cost

The line earns more in fares than it costs to run: £177 million more in 2025/26 (derived).[[gla]] Two cautions. The surplus is about half of what the Authority pays each year on its loan, and the two sit in different accounts. And at most about half of the income is new to TfL: the rest was already paid on its other lines (derived).[[el_til23]]

For Paris the picture is less comfortable. In the central case of this study the new income covers {{ f.central.ops_cover | pct }} of the yearly running cost, but only with the whole income package: the charge on high-speed and regional express passengers, the new Navigo passes, the fee of the regional authority, the shops and half of the operators' saving on trainsets. With the charge on high-speed passengers alone it covers {{ f.tgv_charge_only.ops_cover | pct }}, and with no charge at all {{ f.no_charge.ops_cover | pct }}: see the operations account. How London's supplement serves as a precedent is in the financing precedents.

Benefits against costs

A benefit to cost ratio divides what a project is expected to bring, counted in money over 60 years, by what it costs. Above 1, it gives back more than it costs. "Wider benefits" are gains beyond time saved, mainly the extra output of firms and workers brought closer together; they are harder to estimate.

The same ratio on two bases. British appraisals deduct the fares from the cost before dividing. The 1.97 of 2011 divides the benefits by a cost net of fares of £{{ d.elizabeth.london.pv.capex + d.elizabeth.london.pv.opex - d.elizabeth.london.pv.revenue | 1 }} billion: capital {{ d.elizabeth.london.pv.capex | 1 }}, running {{ d.elizabeth.london.pv.opex | 1 }}, less revenue {{ d.elizabeth.london.pv.revenue | 1 }}, present values in pounds of 2002. User benefits were put at £{{ d.elizabeth.london.pv.benefits_lo | 1 }} billion valued as the national ministry does and £{{ d.elizabeth.london.pv.benefits_hi | 1 }} billion as London does.[[el_bc2011]] This study divides by the gross cost. On that basis the Elizabeth line stands at about {{ d.elizabeth.london.pv.benefits_lo / (d.elizabeth.london.pv.capex + d.elizabeth.london.pv.opex) | 1 }} with user benefits valued as the national ministry does and {{ d.elizabeth.london.pv.benefits_hi / (d.elizabeth.london.pv.capex + d.elizabeth.london.pv.opex) | 1 }} as London does (derived); this project at {{ f.central.econ_cover_all | 2 }} on gross cost and {{ f.central.econ_cover_net | 2 }} on cost net of its income, {{ f.growth.econ_cover_all | 2 }} and {{ f.growth.econ_cover_net | 2 }} if traffic and benefits grow by {{ fp.growth | pct1 }} a year: see the benefits.

What the official evaluation does not show
  • No value for money yet. "It is still too early for an in-depth analysis of the Elizabeth line's value for money."[[el_evalfull]]
  • No result on property or tax. No measured effect on property values or on tax receipts has been published. The figures in circulation, such as "£42 billion added to the economy", are forecasts made by the promoter before the opening.[[el_bc2011, el_tax07, el_gva]]
  • Homes and jobs without a cause. 71,000 homes were built within 1 km of stations from 2015 to 2024, and jobs grew, mostly in the City, where the report cannot tell the line's effect apart.[[tfleval]]

What Paris can take from it, and where the comparison stops

What Paris can take from it

  • The risk is in the stations and the systems, not in the tunnels. Main station contracts tripled or more: Paddington from £181 million to £571 million.[[nao19]]
  • Do not fix the opening date early. The project was "dominated by a fixed completion date" set eight years ahead.[[el_nao19s]] The official lesson: "Use an evidenced range rather than a single target date."[[el_dft19]]
  • Fewer contracts, and one body in charge of the joins. With 36 main contracts and nobody but the client managing the joins between them, the client paid for knock-on delays.[[nao19]]
  • Protect the testing period. Trains switch between three signalling systems.[[nao19]] Preparing the line for service took 14 months against 4 assumed.[[el_ops]]
  • Open in stages, and plan the stages again when delay comes.[[el_ops]] For Paris see the stages.
  • Price the stations with care. London's central stations cost €{{ [s.value for s in d.cost.stations if 'Elizabeth' in s.name][0] | 1 }} million per metre of platform track, twice the €{{ m.rates.platform_m * 1000 | 1 }} million used here. At the London price the three Paris stations would cost about €{{ m.cost.items.Stations * m.gross_up | 0 }} billion more: see the prices used.

Where the comparison stops

  • London built a large metro. One kind of train, 205 m long. Its 24 trains an hour need only two platform tracks per station. No long-distance train uses the tunnel.
  • Paris would take 400 m high-speed trains. They stand longer at the platform than a metro, so Paris Nord-Est needs {{ m.cost.tracks['Paris Nord-Est'] | 0 }} tracks, {{ m.cost.detail['Paris Nord-Est'].hs | 0 }} of them for high-speed trains: see how many platforms. Nobody has built a deep station of that size.
  • London joined two suburban networks. Paris would join four high-speed lines, four regional express corridors and five suburban lines, through three stations instead of one line.
  • London had no fast line across its centre; Paris has five. The RER lines already cross Paris, so the gain for each regional passenger is a second stop, not a new railway. That is why the model counts {{ m.regional.new | 1 }} million new regional journeys a year on {{ m.regional.users | 0 }} million users, where London multiplied its traffic by four.
  • The cost is larger: €{{ m.cost.total | 0 }} billion (€{{ m.cost_low | 0 }} to {{ m.cost_high | 0 }} with low and high prices) against about €32 billion, for {{ m.cost.tunnel_km / d.elizabeth.london.tunnel_km | 1 }} times the tunnel.
  • London's fares pay for running the line. In this study the new income covers {{ f.central.ops_cover | pct }} of the running cost, and only with the whole income package; {{ f.tgv_charge_only.ops_cover | pct }} with the charge on high-speed passengers alone.
  • A planned frequency is not a promise. The line runs 16 trains an hour at quieter times where its sponsors required 20.[[el_tflvalue]]
The reading

The Elizabeth line shows that a through tunnel under a capital fills up fast, relieves the lines beside it and can pay for its own running. It also shows that most of the traffic is moved from other lines, and that stations and systems decide cost and delay. Its evaluators warn against carrying the results to other places.[[el_evalfull]] Zürich, the other precedent, is closer to Paris in kind, with long-distance trains under a working terminal: see Zürich main station.
