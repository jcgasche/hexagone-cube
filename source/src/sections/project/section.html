Three deep TGV stations under the existing ones, joined in a triangle, and a fourth deep station under Saint-Lazare for RER F, a new line that joins today’s suburban lines H, K and L. There are two kinds of tunnel: TGV + TER tunnels, which the TGVs share with the regional express trains, and RER tunnels, for RER G (today’s lines P and N, joined), which stops at Châtelet-Les Halles, and RER F: {{ m.cost.tunnel_km | 1 }} km of twin-track tunnel in all, so that every train of Gare du Nord, Gare de l’Est and Montparnasse, and every regional express train of Gare de Lyon, can leave the surface and their tracks can become parks. Every TGV stops at two of the three stations, and each of the three has direct TGVs to all four directions. Nothing here has been designed: the map and the quantities are planning assumptions.

The words used

A through station is a station where trains arrive at one end and leave by the other, without turning back. A twin-track tunnel carries two tracks, one each way. A corridor is a route followed by one or more such tunnels side by side. A portal is the place where a tunnel comes up to join an existing line. An underground junction is a cavern where two tunnels become one.

The TGV is the French high-speed train. Regional express trains are the TER services that join Paris to the towns of the surrounding regions; a TGV + TER tunnel is a tunnel they share with the TGVs, which have priority. The RER is the set of express suburban lines that cross Paris underground, A to E today. The project adds two: RER F joins today’s suburban lines H, K and L into one line, and RER G joins today’s lines P and N.

Where the tunnels come up

The approach corridors run from each station to the existing tracks at the ring road, where the tunnels come up; on the eastern line they come up at Rosa Parks, just inside it, and RER F comes up near Pont-Cardinet, on the tracks out of Saint-Lazare. A tunnel 35 to 40 m deep needs only about 1.0 to 1.2 km to reach the surface, and a kilometre kept underground costs far more than a kilometre of existing track, so the tunnels go no further.

The triangle of TGV stations

The three TGV stations form a triangle of TGV + TER tunnels: two sides of it run through Paris Nord-Est, the third is the direct link between the two southern stations. Switch each kind of tunnel on and off, and select a station, a corridor, a portal or a junction to read its facts.

Separation by type of train

The plan has two kinds of twin-track tunnel: TGV + TER tunnels, where the regional express trains run between the TGVs, and RER tunnels, one for RER G and one for RER F. There is one TGV + TER tunnel on each side of the triangle: Nord-Est to Gare de Lyon, Nord-Est to Montparnasse, and the direct link between Montparnasse and Gare de Lyon, with the four approaches from the north, the east, the south-east and the Atlantic.

Where two tunnels run side by side

Between Nord-Est and Montparnasse, two tunnels run side by side: the TGV + TER tunnel and the RER G tunnel. Between Nord-Est and Gare de Lyon, and on the link, there is only the TGV + TER tunnel. RER F has an RER tunnel of its own, from the north under Nord-Est to a new deep station under Saint-Lazare, and back up onto the tracks at Pont-Cardinet.

What a path is

A path is a slot in the timetable for one train through a tunnel. In the TGV + TER tunnels the paths are {{ 60 / m.ter_paths.paths_per_hour | 0 }} minutes apart, {{ m.ter_paths.paths_per_hour | 0 }} an hour each way. The TGVs keep their places of the {{ m.stop_min + m.reserve_min + m.clear_min | 0 }}-minute beat, and the regional express trains take the paths in between. TGV times shown to passengers do not change: the timetable shows the time the doors close, and the train may leave up to {{ m.reserve_min | 0 }} minutes later, the reserve in the beat, in its path between the regional trains.

Every train, TGV or regional, takes the same {{ m.run_min | 0 }}-minute run between stations, so none catches up with another. Which trains use which paths is in trains and timetable.

The condition: signalling for trains {{ m.ter_paths.signal_min[0] | 1 }} minutes apart

Sharing works only if trains can follow each other about {{ m.ter_paths.signal_min[0] | 1 }} minutes apart in the tunnels: ETCS level 2, the European cab signalling by radio, with short blocks and automatic driving in the tunnels assumption. The usual rule (UIC 406) keeps a line under {{ m.ter_paths.uic_peak | pct }} of its capacity at the peak. With trains {{ m.ter_paths.signal_min[0] | 1 }} minutes apart, the busiest tunnel, the Atlantic approach, is at {{ m.ter_paths.loads.atlantic.occ_15 | pct }}; if the signalling only reached {{ m.ter_paths.signal_min[1] | 0 }} minutes it would be at {{ m.ter_paths.loads.atlantic.occ_20 | pct }}, and the regional express trains would need a tunnel of their own from Montparnasse to Vanves, {{ m.ter_paths.atlantic_option.km | 1 }} km, about €{{ m.ter_paths.atlantic_option.cost | 1 }} billion all-in. That option is noted, not built.

Why these through lines

Why the kinds of train are kept apart

Separating the kinds of train keeps each tunnel simple: a late suburban train never holds up a TGV, and each suburban tunnel can be signalled like a metro line. The regional express trains are the exception: they share the TGV + TER tunnels, where the TGVs have priority.

What a swap of places costs

As a path comes every {{ m.ter_paths.signal_min[0] | 1 }} to {{ m.ter_paths.signal_min[1] | 0 }} minutes, a regional express train and a TGV that swap places lose less than 3 minutes, the Swiss standard for a train on time,[[ch_sbbfacts]] and less than 5 minutes, the French one assumption. But the through lines were chosen for where they lead, not for the Paris stations themselves; this is the plan’s reasoning.

  • RER G is an employment corridor. The point is not that line P riders reach Montparnasse: they have other ways. Today’s line N serves the research, science, university and company clusters of the south-west, at Versailles, Vélizy and Saint-Quentin-en-Yvelines, with line 18 from Versailles-Chantiers to Saclay. Joining it to line P puts the homes of the north-east and the east much closer to those highly qualified jobs.
  • RER F leads to La Défense. The point is not Saint-Lazare, which RER E already serves. It is a ride without a change from the northern suburbs to Pont-Cardinet, Clichy-Levallois, Courbevoie and La Défense, large job clusters, and it relieves RER E, which is saturated in the morning. It also joins Saint-Lazare and the western suburbs directly to Gare du Nord: the airport connections, line K, and an easy change to RER B towards Roissy, Villepinte and Tremblay-en-France.
  • Suburb to suburb. Trips from one side of the Paris region to the other are rare today because they are nearly impossible, not for lack of demand. Grand Paris Express line 15 was built on that same reasoning.
  • Everything underground at Nord and Est. Taking every line of Gare du Nord and Gare de l’Est underground frees all the surface tracks of both stations for the park; leaving even one line on the surface would keep most of the cost of the tracks and lose the park.

The regional benefit of the station model measures the minutes saved to reach Paris destinations near the second station; the employment corridors beyond the stations are measured on the census commuting flows in the economic return.

Parks where the tracks were

Tracks freed at the north, the east and Montparnasse

Once both stages are open, every train of Gare du Nord, Gare de l’Est and Montparnasse runs underground, and the fans of tracks inside Paris are no longer needed: the two fans north of Gare du Nord and Gare de l’Est, and the tracks from Montparnasse to the ring road. This is why every line of Nord and Est goes underground: leaving even one on the surface would keep the tracks and lose the park.

Gare de Lyon and Bercy

At Gare de Lyon the TGVs and the regional express trains go underground and the Bercy station is freed; only line R keeps a small terminal and a few tracks, so most of the fan towards Bercy can become a park too. The project turns the freed tracks into parks, from the end of each station building outwards, with some small plots kept for new buildings or for reusing the buildings already there.

How many hectares

About {{ m.params.park_ha | 0 }} hectares of track fields, an estimate read from maps assumption; about {{ d.french.params.items.freed_ha.central | 0 }} are freed once the tracks the RER, line R and freight keep are taken out, {{ d.french.parks.central.ha | 0 }} of them as parks and {{ d.french.parks.central.plots_ha | 0 }} as building plots. No official measure of the track corridors exists. The one published figure found is about 30 hectares of rail corridor inside the Bercy-Charenton development area (derived).[[w_bercy]]

What stays

  • The four halls, as entrances to the stations below.
  • The RER and metro stations under them.
  • The surface station of Saint-Lazare, for line J and the Normandy trains.
  • The depots, beyond the ring road, reached through the portals and the existing tracks.

What is not counted

  • No income from the land enters the accounts; the sale of the small plots is counted once in the economic return.
  • The parks are valued in the economic return, by the visits they draw and the health they bring, net of what they cost to clear, make and keep; their area is read from plans and never measured.
  • Clearing the tracks, making the parks and building on the small plots are not in the construction cost of the project; the economic return counts them against the parks and the plots.

Two points are open. The airport express train to Roissy is due to end at surface platforms of Gare de l’Est;[[cdgx]] what becomes of it, when all the surface tracks there are freed, has not been studied. And line R keeps a small surface terminal at Gare de Lyon; running it through later would free a little more land, at about €{{ m.ter_paths.r_option.cost | 1 }} billion; see separation by type of train.
