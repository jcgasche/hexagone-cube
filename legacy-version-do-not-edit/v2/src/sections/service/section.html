The same pattern of trains is assumed every hour. Every high-speed train stops at Paris Nord-Est; trains from the southeast also stop at Gare de Lyon, and trains from the Atlantic at Montparnasse. In a standard busy hour, {{ m.service.through_each_way | 0 }} trains each way run straight through Paris and {{ m.service.reversing_each_way | 0 }} each way pass from one southern line to the other by reversing at Nord-Est. That pattern, with room kept for the day the southern lines are full, sets the number of platforms.

One standard hour of high-speed trains

  • Every train stops at Paris Nord-Est. A train from Bordeaux stops at Montparnasse, then at Nord-Est, then goes on. A train from Lille stops at Nord-Est, then at Gare de Lyon or at Montparnasse. So most trains stop twice in Paris, and every line meets every other at Nord-Est.
  • The same pattern every hour. The study assumes that the same trains run at the same minutes every hour. It has not drawn up a timetable, and the platform count below assumes that stops are spread through the hour. A timetable in which trains wait for each other at Nord-Est, as in Switzerland, is an option; it would need more platform tracks (see the Swiss timetable applied to Paris).
  • Platforms are fixed. The 14:07 to Lyon leaves from the same platform every day, printed in the timetable, with the place of each coach marked on the platform.[[sbb]] What that changes for boarding is in the three stations.
  • High-speed trains are not for trips inside Paris. Tickets are sold from or to a station outside the region.

Any line can be paired with any other

The tunnels join all four lines at Nord-Est, so a train arriving from one line can leave by any of the others. The timetable is free to pair them as traffic requires, and to change the pairing from one hour to the next.

The four lines are not equal. The two southern lines carry more than twice the passengers of the northern and eastern ones: about 44 and 35 million a year counted at their Paris terminals, against 21.5 and 12 (derived).[[art24]] So not every train from the south can find a partner in the north or the east. In the standard hour, {{ m.service.flows['Atlantic to north'] | 0 }} trains from each southern line go on to the north and {{ m.service.flows['Atlantic to east'] | 0 }} to the east, which uses every train of the northern and eastern lines. The {{ m.service.reversing_each_way | 0 }} trains left on each southern line are paired with each other.

For passengers, this gives direct trains on five of the six pairs of lines; only north to east has none. Crossing Paris by TGV gives the times for each pair.

If trains kept to fixed pairs

A simpler timetable would tie the southeast line to the north and the Atlantic line to the east, with no reversing. Direct trains would then exist on two pairs of lines only, and the project would add {{ m.sens['Trains keep to fixed pairs of lines, none reverse'].tgv_journeys | 1 }} million high-speed journeys a year instead of {{ m.yearly.tgv_journeys | 1 }} million. The other tests are in what could change the answer.

A train from Bordeaux stops at Montparnasse and at Nord-Est, changes direction there, stops at Gare de Lyon and continues to Marseille. This balances the four lines, and it gives direct trains between the Atlantic and the southeast, the largest flow across Paris.

  • How long it takes. A TGV needs 10 to 12 minutes to reverse at a platform, or about 3 with a second driver waiting at the other end.[[fnaut]] The estimate allows {{ m.reverse | 0 }}, and counts {{ m.reverse + 1 | 0 }} minutes of platform time: one more for the train to clear the platform. The times between stations ({{ m.TH.L | 0 }} and {{ m.TH.M | 0 }} minutes, stop included) are assumptions too.
  • What it costs. Platform time: reversals take up {{ (2 * m.service.reversing_each_way + m.service.spare) * m.service.rev_equiv / m.service.nordest_stops | pct }} of the platform time at Nord-Est in the standard hour. That is one reason why this station is the largest.
  • What it is worth to passengers. Less than a through run: {{ m.connecting.central.pairs['Montparnasse-Lyon'].direct | 0 }} minutes in Paris against {{ m.connecting.central.pairs['Montparnasse-Nord'].direct | 0 }} for a train that runs straight through, but still about half of today's {{ m.connecting.central.pairs['Montparnasse-Lyon'].old_clock | 1 }} minutes and with no change of train.
  • Other ways to handle the surplus remain open: sidings beside the high-speed depot 2 km north of Gare du Nord (unverified),[[landy]] or running on to the airport station at Roissy. Neither is costed here.

How many platforms

Through stations are planned for four to six long-distance trains an hour on each platform track. Stuttgart's new station, which is planned and not yet open, reached six in a simulated stress test,[[bt19, dhz]] and the airport station at Roissy is rated at 8 trains an hour each way on its 4 tracks in theory.[[rffcdg]] One station in service gives a measure: Zürich's Löwenstrasse station handles about 460 trains a day on four tracks,[[zvv]] and through trains that wait for connections there stand 6 to 9 minutes (derived from the departure boards; see Zürich).[[zh_board_arr, zh_board_dep]] The rule used here: {{ m.per_track | 0 }} stops of {{ (m.reverse + 1) / m.service.rev_equiv | 0 }} minutes an hour on each track, so that a track is free two thirds of the time and a late train finds its platform. A train that reverses holds its platform for {{ m.reverse + 1 | 0 }} minutes, {{ m.reverse | 0 }} to change direction and 1 to clear, and counts as {{ m.service.rev_equiv | 2 }} stops. The stations are sized for the day the southern lines are full.

The sliders change the rule, not the timetable: more stops an hour on each track means less slack when trains run late.
