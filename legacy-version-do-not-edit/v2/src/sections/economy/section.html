Most of what the project returns never passes through a ticket gate: time saved by passengers of the TGV (the French high-speed train) and of regional trains, more comfortable journeys, a larger job market. Counted with the official French values, these benefits are worth €{{ f.A_central.benefit_total | 2 }} billion a year, {{ f.A_central.econ_cover_all | pct }} to {{ f.B_central.econ_cover_all | pct }} of the yearly cost. Comparable tunnels were approved with benefits between half and twice their cost. As counted here, this project is below that range, and this chapter shows what is missing.

  • Layer 1: money that reaches the Treasury because of the project. Tickets carry 10% VAT, which businesses cannot recover.[[eco_vat]] It applies to the net change in ticket money. With the charge, high-speed traffic does not rise, so this layer is about zero; the charge itself is taken as free of VAT. VAT lost on what passengers stop buying elsewhere is not deducted. Experience elsewhere is that this layer can be negative: the evaluation of High Speed 1 found a loss of tax of over £0.8 billion, because rail travel is taxed less than what it replaces.[[hs1]]
  • Layer 2: tax on added activity. France takes 43.6% of its output in taxes and social contributions.[[eco_insee]] The rate is not in doubt; what is in doubt is how much of a local gain is new for the country. British guidance starts from the assumption that none is, unless the project is shown to bring more people into work.[[eco_tag21, eco_tag23]] That is the first column. The middle column takes the wider effects as the appraisal of the Grand Paris Express counts them: each extra job is valued there at €10,000 a year of unemployment benefits saved,[[sgpi]] which is itself a public saving; output from denser job markets and from working time saved (for the {{ b.tax.business_pax | pct }} of TGV passengers who travel for work, unverified[[eco_biz]]) is taxed at 43.6%. The last column supposes that the {{ round(f.A_central.jobs, -2) | int }} jobs implied are all new and each pays tax on the average output of a job in the region, €123,220.[[eco_gdp]]
  • Layer 3: tax returned by the works. A simulation by the French economic observatory finds that 40% to 60% of debt-financed public investment returns to public budgets within five years, through wages, profits and lower benefits.[[eco_ofce]] This is real cash for the Treasury while the works last. It is not a gain from this project: any spending of the same size would return the same.
How official appraisal treats taxes

The French method does not count taxes as a benefit. It counts each euro of public money at €1.20, because raising taxes has a cost of its own, and it leaves out jobs created by the works and multiplier effects to avoid counting twice.[[eco_guide]] The builder of the Grand Paris Express claimed up to €4 billion of tax a year for output of €10 billion to €20 billion; the State's reviewers did not endorse a tax line.[[eco_senat44, sgpi]] For Crossrail, consultants put tax at 27% to 29% of added output.[[eco_volterra]] The table above is therefore a view for public finances, beside the appraisal and not inside it.

Against comparable projects

The measure used everywhere is the ratio of benefits to costs over the life of the project, both brought to today's value. Above 1, the country gets back more than it spends. The ratios below are not strictly comparable, and the differences cut both ways:

  • This project is worked out over 60 years at a discount rate of 3%, with benefits growing {{ fp.growth | pct1 }} a year assumption, no value left at the end, running costs added to the cost, and no extra weight on public money.
  • French appraisals (Grand Paris Express, Marseille) use 4.5%, count effects to 2140 and weigh public money at 1.2. At 4.5% over 60 years this project's ratio falls to {{ f.A_rate45.econ_cover_all | 2 }} for layout A and {{ f.B_rate45.econ_cover_all | 2 }} for layout B.
  • British appraisals deduct ticket revenue from the cost, which raises the ratio: the Elizabeth line's 1.97 would be about 0.9 if its running costs were added to the cost as they are here (derived from its published present values).[[eco_cr11]]
  • Australian appraisals use a 7% rate, which lowers theirs.[[eco_ia17]]

What the comparison says

  • On transport benefits alone, cross-city tunnels rarely return much more than their cost. Published ratios at approval run from about 0.5 to 2. Five of the thirteen stand below 1 on those benefits; they were built or are promoted for capacity, or rest on wider effects: Stockholm, Malmö, Gothenburg, Lucerne, the Grand Paris Express.
  • Wider effects decide the largest projects. They are a fifth to a third of the benefits in London, Sydney and Melbourne, and about 60% for the Grand Paris Express.
  • Measured returns are usually lower than forecast. High Speed 1 went from 1.5 to 0.64, Stockholm from 0.86 to 0.51, RER E from 21% to about 10%.
  • This project is below all of them as counted here, on any of the bases above. It costs more than any of them and its counted benefits are those of a mid-sized scheme. Either the count misses its main purpose, capacity for more trains, or the project is too large for what it delivers. Telling the two apart is the first job of a study.

What would have to be true

Could the benefits reach the cost? Not in any case tested. Each step below adds one favourable assumption to the one before.

Closing the rest would take benefits that are not counted here. Three could be large enough, and each can be checked:

  1. Capacity. If the terminals are what stops more trains from running, the project should be credited with the traffic it unlocks. For scale: each 10% of extra high-speed traffic is {{ m.extras.capacity_journeys | 0 }} million journeys and €{{ m.extras.capacity_revenue * 1000 | 0 }} million of tickets a year, {{ m.extras.capacity_revenue / f.A_central.yearly_cost | pct }} to {{ m.extras.capacity_revenue / f.B_central.yearly_cost | pct }} of the yearly cost. Even counted in full, the gap of the central case would take about {{ (f.B_central.yearly_cost - f.B_central.benefit_total) / m.extras.capacity_revenue * 10 | 0 }}% more traffic for layout B, against {{ m.extras.south_growth | pct }} more trains planned on the southern lines. This needs the view of the track owner, SNCF Réseau, of when each terminal and each line is full, with and without the project.
  2. The regional network. The count for suburban passengers rests on an average saving of {{ m.regional.avg_clock_saving | 1 }} minutes and on ratios borrowed from another project. The region's travel model would show suburb to suburb trips, relief of the RER, and a real figure for wider effects.
  3. Cost. Stations at the Zürich price would cut the cost of layout A by {{ m.cost.A.items.Stations * (1 - 0.55) * m.gross_up / m.cost.A.total | pct }}. Whether Paris ground and 400 m trains allow it is an engineering question.
