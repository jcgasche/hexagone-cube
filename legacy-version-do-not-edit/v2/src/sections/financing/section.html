The money is kept in two accounts. Running the tunnels and stations would cost about €{{ f.A_central.ops_cost_total | 2 }} billion a year; a charge of €{{ fp.charge_end | 2 }} on each high-speed passenger and the other income found here cover {{ f.A_central.ops_cover | pct }} to {{ f.B_central.ops_cover | pct }} of it, and between half and all of it across what running costs could turn out to be. Repaying construction costs €{{ f.B_central.annuity | 2 }} billion to €{{ f.A_central.annuity | 2 }} billion a year and nothing the project earns reaches that: it has to come from taxes and grants, as for most comparable tunnels.

Two accounts

Two words first: TGV is the French high-speed train, and RER the express suburban lines that cross Paris underground. An operations account holds what it costs each year to keep the tunnels and stations open, and what the project earns each year. A capital account holds the repayment of construction, spread over 60 years like a loan, and the taxes and grants assigned to it. Keeping them apart answers two different questions: can the project pay its own way once built, and who pays to build it.

The running cost is the least certain figure

Two French records exist, and they are far apart.

  • The first RER E section (two deep stations and 4 km of tunnel, opened in 1999) cost €21.3 million a year in 2003 to maintain and operate, several times its forecast: 8.7 for maintenance of the infrastructure, 9.2 for operating the stations, 3.4 for guarding and utilities.[[cgpc]] Brought to today with the public-works index, that is €13,200 a year per metre of platform track and about €3.5 million per km of tunnel. The index is a choice: no official index exists for running costs, and consumer prices have risen less.
  • The RER network run by RATP costs more than €0.5 million per km of track a year to maintain, so about €1.0 million per km of two-track tunnel.[[fin_art24rer]]
  • Used here: stations at the RER E rate; tunnels at €{{ m.params.om_tunnel * 1000 | 2 }} million per km, midway between the two assumption. The low case takes tunnels at the RER network rate and stations at {{ fp.station_low | pct }} of the RER E rate; the high case takes both from the RER E record.
  • Gross, not net. The new stations replace terminals whose staff, cleaning and energy are paid for today. That saving is not deducted, because it has not been measured. It would lower the cost again.
  • The level of the charge. In the central case operations balance at €{{ f.A_breakeven_charge | 2 }} per departure or arrival for layout A and €{{ f.B_breakeven_charge | 2 }} for layout B; at the low end of running costs, at €{{ f.A_breakeven_charge_low_running | 2 }} and €{{ f.B_breakeven_charge_low_running | 2 }}; at the high end, at €{{ f.A_breakeven_charge_high_running | 2 }} and €{{ f.B_breakeven_charge_high_running | 2 }}. A higher charge loses more passengers.
  • More sources. Those that could be negotiated add about €{{ (f.A_with_options.ops_rev_total - f.A_central.ops_rev_total) | m }} million a year.
  • What to ask first. The track owner and the station operator hold the real running costs of RER E, of the stations opened in 2024 and of today's terminals. They decide whether this account balances.

For comparison: fares cover 37% of the running of public transport in the Paris region (derived)[[fin_idfmbud26]] and 64% in the Zürich network.[[fin_zvv24]] The Elizabeth line earned £731 million in fares against £554 million of running costs in 2025 to 2026, trains included;[[gla]] it keeps its fares. Here the fares of high-speed trains belong to their operators and suburban fares to the region, which is why the project needs a charge of its own.

A charge on high-speed passengers

The proposal tested: every TGV ticket to or from Paris carries €{{ fp.charge_end | 2 }}, the price of a metro ticket in 2026.[[fares26]] A passenger who arrives in Paris and leaves on another train pays it twice. A passenger who crosses Paris on a direct train pays €{{ fp.charge_through | 2 }}. The reasoning is that passengers from the Paris region gain a choice of stations, and passengers who cross Paris gain a great deal of time.

What it brings. About {{ b.traffic.central.paris_base + b.traffic.central.cross_base | 0 }} million high-speed journeys a year touch Paris: {{ b.traffic.central.paris_base | 0 }} million start or end there and {{ b.traffic.central.cross_base | 1 }} million cross it. After the loss of traffic described below, the charge collects €{{ f.A_central.tgv.charge_gross * 1000 | 0 }} million a year. The track owner loses the charges it collects on the journeys given up, which leaves €{{ f.A_central.tgv.charge_net * 1000 | 0 }} million.

What it costs in traffic. €{{ fp.charge_end | 2 }} is {{ fp.charge_end / b.fare | pct }} on an average fare of €{{ b.fare | 1 }} before VAT. French experts take traffic to fall 0.7% when fares rise 1%, perhaps 0.5% where prices already vary with demand, as they do on TGVs.[[cgedd_fares]] On that basis the charge loses {{ f.A_resp05.tgv.lost | 1 }} million to {{ f.A_central.tgv.lost | 1 }} million journeys a year. The project adds {{ f.A_central.tgv.new_before_charge | 1 }} million. With this charge, high-speed traffic through Paris ends {{ 0 - f.A_resp05.tgv.net_new | 1 }} million to {{ 0 - f.A_central.tgv.net_new | 1 }} million journeys lower than today.

Why. The gain is uneven. A passenger who crosses Paris saves about {{ b.traffic.central.clock_cross | 0 }} minutes on the clock. The official method counts a change at twice its length, which makes {{ b.traffic.central.eq_cross | 0 }} minutes, worth about €{{ f.A_central.tgv.gain_cross_euro | 0 }}: far more than €{{ fp.charge_through | 2 }}. A passenger from the Paris region gains {{ b.traffic.central.clock_access | 1 }} minutes on the clock on average, counted as {{ b.traffic.central.eq_access | 1 }} with the official weights on walking and waiting, worth about €{{ f.A_central.tgv.gain_paris_euro | 2 }}: less than the charge. And there are twenty times more of the second kind.

A variant that follows the gains. Charging €{{ fp.charge_alt[0] | 2 }} per departure or arrival and €{{ fp.charge_alt[1] | 0 }} for a direct crossing keeps traffic at today's level (change of {{ f.A_charge_matched.tgv.net_new | 1 }} million journeys) but collects €{{ f.A_charge_matched.tgv.charge_gross * 1000 | 0 }} million instead of €{{ f.A_central.tgv.charge_gross * 1000 | 0 }} million. The volume is in Paris trip ends, so any design must choose between money and traffic. Both variants can be tried in the calculator.

Is it possible in law? Two routes exist. Station charges are regulated at cost plus a return: €1,260 million a year for all French stations in 2026.[[fin_art069]] The cost of the new stations would enter that base and be billed to train operators, who pass it on. European law also lets a track owner charge more for a specific project, on the basis of its long-term cost.[[fin_dir2012]] The other route is a levy written into a budget law, as for the airport express, repaid in part by a levy on air passengers.[[fin_cdgxlevy, cdgx2]] Precedents for the level: the solidarity tax on a flight from Paris within Europe is €7.40;[[fin_airtax]] operators pay the Channel Tunnel about €35 per passenger (derived, upper bound).[[fin_getlink]] The figures here take the charge as free of VAT; if it carried the 10% VAT of tickets, the project would keep €{{ fp.charge_end / 1.1 | 2 }} of each €{{ fp.charge_end | 2 }}.

More Navigo pass holders

Navigo is the travel pass of the Paris region. Joining the suburban lines through Paris should bring new regular users: the model expects {{ m.regional.new_transilien | 1 }} million more suburban journeys a year (see regional traffic). A pass costs €998.80 a year whatever the number of trips,[[fares26]] so a pass holder who travels more brings nothing. What counts is how many people buy a pass because of the project.

The estimate. A regular commuter makes about {{ b.pass_trips | 0 }} rail journeys a year assumption. If {{ fp.navigo_new | pct }} of the new journeys are made by new pass holders assumption, that is about {{ round(f.A_central.pass_holders, -2) | int }} passes and €{{ f.A_central.ops_rev['New Navigo pass holders'] | m }} million a year. The share is the unknown. Where it has been measured on new through lines, the share of journeys that are new to public transport was 9% to 10% in London,[[tfleval]] 27% in Sydney (derived)[[sydney]] and 28% to 32% on the East London line;[[fin_ng11]] the figure used here is at the top of that range.

How to measure it. Count passes and validations on lines H, K, P, N and R before and after opening, and compare with lines that did not change. Passes are registered to a home address, so new holders along the joined lines can be counted directly. The regional authority's budget attributes no revenue to new lines today;[[fin_idfmbud26]] an agreement would have to say that the measured gain is paid to the project.

Its weight. Small either way. Fares bring the region €4.2 billion a year,[[fin_idfmbud26]] and the project adds a few tens of millions at most. The extra running of the trains, €{{ b.om.trains * 1000 | 0 }} million a year, is charged to the project in the same account.
