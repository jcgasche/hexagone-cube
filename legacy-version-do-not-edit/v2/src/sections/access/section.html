From the average point in Paris, a station where your TGV, the French high-speed train, stops is {{ pts.avg.today | 1 }} minutes away today by public transport. With the three through stations it is {{ pts.avg.station | 1 }} minutes to the nearest station where the train stops, or {{ pts.avg.ride | 1 }} minutes if the train’s ride between its Paris stops is counted, the measure used for benefits. The stations stay where they are and the project only adds places to board, so no point in Paris is further from its train than today, for any direction.

    What “with the project” counts

    Today each direction has one terminal: Gare du Nord for the north, Gare de l’Est for the east, Gare de Lyon for the southeast, Montparnasse for the Atlantic. With the project every high-speed train stops at two or three stations in Paris, and a passenger boards at whichever suits best. Today’s station is always one of them. That is why the time can only get shorter.

    Which trains run where is set out in how the lines are paired and trains that change direction. The even split between the two southern lines is an assumption, and so is free entry to Paris Nord-Est from both halls. The second one matters: with entry from the Gare du Nord side only, the Paris average on the stricter measure below would be {{ m.paris.nord_hall | 1 }} minutes and not {{ pts.avg.ride | 1 }}, and people who live nearer to Gare de l’Est would be further from a train for the east than today.

    There are two honest ways to count the result. The map offers both.

    To the station entrance

    {{ pts.avg.station | 1 }} minutes on average, {{ pts.avg.today - pts.avg.station | 1 }} fewer than today.

    The minutes by public transport from a point in Paris to the entrance of the nearest station where the train stops. Where only half of the trains stop at the nearest station, the map takes the average of the two halves.

    This answers the plain question: how far am I from a station where my train stops? On this count {{ pts.share_quicker.station | pct }} of the points are quicker than today, by up to {{ pts.max_gain.station | 1 }} minutes.

    Counting the ride across Paris

    {{ pts.avg.ride | 1 }} minutes on average, {{ pts.avg.today - pts.avg.ride | 1 }} fewer than today.

    The same, plus the train’s ride between its Paris stops when you board at the far one: {{ pts.th.L | 0 }} minutes between Nord-Est and Gare de Lyon, {{ pts.th.M | 0 }} between Nord-Est and Montparnasse, and {{ pts.reverse | 0 }} more when the train changes direction at Nord-Est assumption. You board at the far stop only when that still puts you ahead.

    This answers a stricter question: how much sooner am I on my way? It is the measure used for benefits. On this count {{ pts.share_quicker.ride | pct }} of the points are quicker, by up to {{ pts.max_gain.ride | 1 }} minutes, and the rest are unchanged.

    An example. Someone who lives near Montparnasse and travels to Lille crosses Paris by metro to Gare du Nord today. With the project, half of the Lille trains come up from the Atlantic and stop at Montparnasse first. The first measure counts only the short trip to Montparnasse. The second adds the {{ pts.th.M | 0 }} minutes the train takes to reach Nord-Est, because until then this passenger is no nearer to Lille than someone who boards at Nord-Est. Those minutes are spent in a seat on the right train and not in the metro, which is worth something, but they are still minutes.

    The model uses the stricter measure

    Every benefit and every extra passenger counted in this study comes from the second measure, the one that counts the train’s ride between its Paris stops. The first measure is shown because it is the one a passenger feels at the door. It is not used to count benefits.

    The map does not include the time inside the station, from the entrance to the coach, which is a separate matter treated in boarding in a deep station; the model also tests what happens if every passenger needs two more minutes than today, in the sensitivity tests.

    How the 1,000 times were computed. A grid of 1,000 points, 315 m apart, covers Paris without its two woods. From each point the model finds the shortest time to the hall of each station on foot, by metro, RER and tram, with the average wait for each line, on the network of Île-de-France Mobilités as of 5 October 2026.[[metropolist]] Buses, taxis and crowding are not counted. The four directions are weighted by their share of high-speed traffic: Atlantic {{ pts.w.Montparnasse | pct }}, southeast {{ pts.w.Lyon | pct }}, north {{ pts.w.Nord | pct }}, east {{ pts.w.Est | pct }} (derived).[[art24]] The full method is in how access times are computed.

    Who gains, and what it is worth

    Every direction gains. At the station entrance the Atlantic and the southeast gain most, because all their trains would also stop at Nord-Est, on the other side of the city from their own station. Once the ride is counted the north gains most: a train for Lille boarded at Gare de Lyon or Montparnasse runs under Paris in {{ pts.th.L | 0 }} or {{ pts.th.M | 0 }} minutes, quicker than the metro.

    The numbers by arrondissement

    The suburbs gain a little more than Paris, because their trains and RER lines arrive on one side of the city. On the stricter measure the model finds {{ -m.access.central.clock.rings['Paris'] | 1 }} minutes saved for a trip that starts in Paris, {{ -m.access.central.clock.rings['Petite couronne'] | 1 }} in the inner suburbs and {{ -m.access.central.clock.rings['Grande couronne'] | 1 }} in the outer suburbs. The map shows Paris only.

    What quicker access is worth in the model

    The model counts the whole Paris region, on the stricter measure, in “equivalent minutes”: the official French method counts a minute of walking or changing as two and a minute of waiting as one and a half, because passengers dislike them more than sitting in a train.[[dgitm]] On that scale the average trip to the train is {{ -m.access_effect.central.minutes | 1 }} minutes shorter.

    That is worth €{{ m.yearly.tgv_access_benefit | m }} million a year in time saved and brings about {{ m.yearly.tgv_access_journeys | 1 }} million more high-speed journeys a year: British ticket data give about 11% more traffic for 10% less journey time.[[ww]]

    Almost all of it comes from the choice of station. With the same three stations but each train boarded only at its own, the gain would be €{{ m.access_effect.no_choice.benefit | m }} million a year. The choice between two places to board is worth €{{ m.value_of_choice.eq | m }} million a year.

    These journeys are part of the high-speed traffic forecast, and the time saved is one line of the benefits.
