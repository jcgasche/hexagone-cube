Every long-distance train to Paris ends in a dead-end station. This study tests replacing the four terminals used by high-speed trains by three deep through stations, joined by tunnels, so that high-speed, regional and suburban trains run across the city. It serves passengers better and nobody is further from a train than today; it would cost €{{ m.cost.B.total | 0 }} billion to €{{ m.cost.A.total | 0 }} billion; and on the benefits that can be counted so far it does not pay for itself.

The findings

Passengers

Reaching a train gets quicker everywhere in Paris. Most trains stop at two stations, so the average time to a station where your train stops falls from {{ pts.avg.today | 1 }} to {{ pts.avg.station | 1 }} minutes. The stations stay where they are: no point loses.

Crossing Paris

{{ m.conn.central | 1 }} million passengers a year stop changing stations by metro. A change that takes 57.5 minutes today becomes a direct train or a change of platform. Regional trains run on to a second station.

Financing

Running costs could be covered; construction cannot. The two accounts at a glance

What this means

As counted here, the project is a public investment paid by taxes and grants (€{{ f.B_central.grant_bn | 0 }} billion to €{{ f.A_central.grant_bn | 0 }} billion of grants on top of a new tax on businesses), with a return well below that of comparable tunnels. That is not yet a case for building, and not a case against studying: the count leaves out the reason most cities gave for through stations, capacity for more trains, and rests on prices and running costs taken from very few records. Five quick tests would confirm or stop the case.

How to use this document

The chapters follow the argument: the problem, what would be built, how it would run, what passengers gain, what it costs, who pays, what the country gets back, what could change the answer. Each can be read alone. Blue links with an arrow jump to the place where a point is treated; small numbers open the source.

Most figures can be explored: move a price in the cost chapter, a charge or a tax in the financing calculator, tap a point on the access map, pair two lines in crossing Paris, step through the works in the timeline.

Words used throughout. TGV: the French high-speed train. RER: the express suburban lines that already cross Paris underground. Layout A gives high-speed trains their own tunnels; layout B has them share with regional express trains.

Three annexes explain the precedents the project draws on: how Zürich turned a terminus into a through station under a working hall, what the Elizabeth line changed in London and how it was paid for, and how the Swiss timetable makes every train meet every train.

Every number comes from a small model described in method and assumptions, or from a document listed in the sources. Assumptions are marked assumption, drawings that are not designs illustrative sketch. No station has been designed; this is a first test of whether the idea is worth a study.
