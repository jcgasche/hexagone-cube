The same pattern of trains is assumed every hour, and no train reverses in Paris. A high-speed train runs from the north or the east to one of the two southern stations through Paris Nord-Est, or between the two southern stations by the direct link, so each train stops at two of the three stations. In a standard busy hour, {{ m.service.through_each_way | 0 }} trains each way run from the north or east to the south and {{ m.service.link_each_way | 0 }} each way run between Montparnasse and Gare de Lyon by the link. That pattern, with room kept for the day the southern lines are full, sets the number of platforms.

One standard hour of high-speed trains

  • Every train stops twice in Paris, and never turns. A train from Bordeaux stops at Montparnasse, then either at Nord-Est, going on to Lille or Strasbourg, or at Gare de Lyon by the direct link, going on to Marseille. A train from Lille stops at Nord-Est, then at Gare de Lyon or at Montparnasse. Every station has direct trains to all four directions, on half of the trains of each line.
  • The same pattern every hour. The study assumes that the same trains run at the same minutes every hour. It has not drawn up a timetable, and the platform count below assumes that stops are spread through the hour. A timetable in which trains wait for each other at Nord-Est, as in Switzerland, is an option; it would need more platform tracks (see the Swiss timetable applied to Paris).
  • Platforms are fixed. The 14:07 to Lyon leaves from the same platform every day, printed in the timetable, with the place of each coach marked on the platform.[[sbb]] What that changes for boarding is in the three stations.
  • High-speed trains are not for trips inside Paris. Tickets are sold from or to a station outside the region.

Any line can be paired with any other

The tunnels join all four lines, so a train arriving from one line can leave by any of the others: the northern and eastern lines meet the two southern ones at Nord-Est, and the two southern lines meet each other by the link. The timetable is free to pair them as traffic requires, and to change the pairing from one hour to the next. The choice holds at every station: from each of the three, there are direct trains in all four directions.

The four lines are not equal. The two southern lines carry more than twice the passengers of the northern and eastern ones: about 44 and 35 million a year counted at their Paris terminals, against 21.5 and 12 (derived).[[art24]] So not every train from the south can find a partner in the north or the east. In the standard hour, {{ m.service.flows['Atlantic to north'] | 0 }} trains from each southern line go on to the north and {{ m.service.flows['Atlantic to east'] | 0 }} to the east, which uses every train of the northern and eastern lines. The {{ m.service.link_each_way | 0 }} trains left on each southern line are paired with each other by the direct link, with no reversal. When the southern lines are full, {{ m.service_full.link_each_way | 0 }} an hour each way take the link and one south-east train an hour is left without a partner: it turns back at Nord-Est, the only train in the pattern that does.

For passengers, this gives direct trains on five of the six pairs of lines; only north to east has none, because the two lines meet just north of Nord-Est. Crossing Paris by TGV gives the times for each pair. Fixed pairs, with each southern line tied to one northern line, would be simpler to run but would leave only two pairs with direct trains; the model no longer tests that case, since the link makes it unnecessary.

How many platforms

The rule. Platforms are dedicated by direction: the tracks on one side of a station take trains heading south, those on the other side trains heading north, as at any through station. A stop takes {{ m.stop_min | 0 }} minutes; after a departure the track needs about {{ m.clear_min | 0 }} minutes before the next train can pull in; and a timetable can use about {{ m.util | pct }} of that {{ m.stop_min + m.clear_min | 0 }}-minute cycle and still absorb late trains. One track therefore takes about {{ m.per_track | 1 }} trains an hour assumption, all three figures. Through stations elsewhere give the measure: Stuttgart's new station reached about six trains an hour on each track in a simulated stress test,[[bt19, dhz]] Zürich's Löwenstrasse handles about 460 trains a day on four tracks, about six an hour on each on average,[[zvv]] and Tokyo's Shinagawa was built with four tracks for 15 trains an hour each way.[[shinagawa_cap]] Through trains that wait for connections in Zürich stand 6 to 9 minutes (derived from the departure boards; see Zürich).[[zh_board_arr, zh_board_dep]]

Three tracks each way where two would do on paper. With the lines full, each station sees {{ m.service_full.stops_direction['Paris Nord-Est'] | 1 }} to {{ m.service_full.stops_direction['Gare de Lyon'] | 0 }} stops an hour in each direction. Two tracks each way would take them: a train every 8 minutes on each, 4 minutes stopped and 4 empty. The third track each way takes the late trains and the trains that stand longer. The stations are sized for the day the southern lines are full. Two-minute stops, which would make two tracks comfortable, are ruled out: passengers with luggage do not board and alight a 400 m train in two minutes. Four-track stations are ruled out for the same reason, as too tight for late trains.

The sliders change the rule, not the timetable: a longer stop, a longer clearing time or a lower share of the hour used all mean fewer trains on each track.

Regional, suburban and London trains

  • Regional express trains have their own platforms, {{ m.len.ter | 0 }} m long: {{ m.cost.detail['Paris Nord-Est'].ter | 0 }} tracks at Nord-Est and {{ m.cost.detail['Gare de Lyon'].ter | 0 }} at each southern station, {{ m.cost.detail['Paris Nord-Est'].ter + m.cost.detail['Gare de Lyon'].ter + m.cost.detail['Montparnasse'].ter | 0 }} in all. The length takes three coupled Regio 2N sets, which are 81 to 135 m each (unverified). The count is the model's, not a timetable's: how many regional express trains an hour each line would run, and whether {{ m.cost.detail['Paris Nord-Est'].ter + m.cost.detail['Gare de Lyon'].ter + m.cost.detail['Montparnasse'].ter | 0 }} platforms is the right number, is study point I3 assumption.
  • Suburban trains have {{ m.cost.detail['Paris Nord-Est'].sub | 0 }} tracks at Nord-Est and {{ m.cost.detail['Gare de Lyon'].sub | 0 }} at each southern station, {{ m.len.sub | 0 }} m long, on the second pair of tunnels assumption. One imbalance needs attention: line H runs about four times as many trains as line R, its partner across Paris (476 against 120 a weekday, unverified).[[wikilines]] Most line H trains would turn back south of Gare de Lyon; the cost includes sidings for that. Whether one pair of tunnels is enough for the peak of lines H, K and P together is study point I4; a third pair of tunnels would cost about 10 billion euros more at this study's unit prices assumption.
  • London trains board from one of the six high-speed tracks at Nord-Est, closed off for each departure, with a larger control hall and waiting room above it (counted at €{{ m.rates.london_hall * 1000 | 0 }} million of works assumption). At about one departure an hour and 10 to 15 minutes of boarding, that fits within the rule above. Amsterdam already boards London trains this way, from one closed-off platform.[[amsterdam]] Trains arriving from London are checked before departure, so they can run on to the south like any other. Northbound, passengers boarding at a southern station for London would need to be checked before boarding: how, and where, is open (study point I5). Today's cross-Channel terminal at Gare du Nord is itself at its limits.[[an14775]]
  • Depots stay where they are; trains reach them through the tunnel portals (see changes on the surface).

Regional express and suburban trains through Paris

The second pair of tunnels joins the suburban lines two by two: lines H and K from the north run on to Gare de Lyon and continue as line R to the south-east; line P from the east runs on to Montparnasse and continues as line N to the west. Regional express trains use the first pair, with the TGVs: those from the north and the east run on to Gare de Lyon or Montparnasse, those from the south-east and the Atlantic to Nord-Est.
