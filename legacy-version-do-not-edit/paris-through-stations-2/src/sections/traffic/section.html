The project would add about {{ m.yearly.tgv_journeys | 1 }} million high-speed journeys a year and {{ m.yearly.reg_journeys | 0 }} million suburban and regional ones. Both figures rest on what was counted in cities that joined their terminals, and on a model of time saved where no count exists. They are small against today's traffic, and a charge on passengers of the TGV, the French high-speed train, would take away more high-speed journeys than the project adds.

What other cities saw

Many cities have joined suburban lines through their centre and counted the passengers afterwards. For long-distance trains the record is thin: no railway has published its passengers before and after a terminal became a through station.

  • The best cases gained 24% to 30% within one to three years. Stockholm's commuter trains gained 26% in two years while the rest of the network gained 3%.[[sl]] Zürich gained 24% in the first year of its 1990 through station, with twice the trains.[[sma]] Tokyo's Fukutoshin line gained 30% in six months.[[toyo13]]
  • Others gained little. Line E of the Paris RER, the express suburban lines that cross Paris underground, has gained about 10% so far on 30% more service.[[art25]] St. Gallen ran 25% more trains for 5% more travel.[[sg]] Philadelphia joined its two networks in 1984 during a collapse of service and saw no gain for ten years (unverified).[[philly]]
  • Where a whole new line came with it, traffic multiplied, mostly by moving passengers. London's Elizabeth line joined two suburban services through a new tunnel: 56 million journeys on the two in 2019/20, 220 million in 2023/24 and 242 million in 2025/26.[[til24, gla]] What made it different from a joined pair of terminal lines: new stations in the centre of the city, where the demand is, and far more trains, 24 an hour through the core. Only 9% to 10% of its journeys are new to public transport;[[tfleval]] in Sydney the figure is 27%.[[sydney]] The annex on the Elizabeth line has the detail.

Reading. A second stop in the city draws a real share of passengers, and removing a change of station is worth far more than the minutes it saves. But no case isolates the effect of a through station on long-distance traffic. That is why this study leans on British ticket data and on a model of time saved.

High-speed trains: two gains, both counted cautiously

Two groups of TGV passengers gain from the project, and each is estimated in its own way.

Starting or ending in the Paris region

{{ m.access_effect.central.idf | 0 }} million journeys a year today. With two stops in Paris for every train, the station where the train stops is nearer for most travellers: {{ -m.access_effect.central.minutes | 1 }} minutes nearer on average, with walking and waiting counted at their official weights and the ride between the two Paris stops counted against the passenger (see reaching the station).

The time model turns that into traffic: about 11% more for a journey 10% shorter.[[ww]] Result: {{ m.access_effect.central.journeys | 1 }} million more journeys, a gain of {{ m.access_effect.central.journeys / m.access_effect.central.idf | pct1 }}. If passengers pick the train that calls at their best station, which the timetable allows since every station has trains to every direction, the saving is {{ -m.access_effect.pick.minutes | 1 }} minutes and the gain {{ m.access_effect.pick.journeys | 1 }} million; the central case keeps the stricter measure.

Crossing Paris

{{ m.conn.central | 1 }} million journeys a year today (derived from the 2012-13 station survey, the last count made; a fresh one is study point I12, see crossing Paris). For the {{ m.connecting.central.direct_share | pct }} who would find a direct train, a gain of {{ m.params.uplift_direct | pct }} is taken, from British ticket data on what a change of train costs (see below).[[ww]] For the others, who still change but inside one station, the time model is used.

Result: {{ m.yearly.tgv_conn_journeys | 1 }} million more journeys, a gain of {{ m.connecting.central.uplift | pct }}. Trains from London, Brussels, Amsterdam and Cologne can run on to the south like any other, since their passengers are checked before departure; northbound checks are open (study point I5).

Why {{ m.params.uplift_direct | pct }}. The British paper finds that adding a change of train loses {{ m.params.uplift_direct | pct }} of passengers in the long run, on routes of about 100 miles.[[ww]] This study takes {{ m.params.uplift_direct | pct }} as the gain when a change is removed assumption. That is cautious: the same finding read the other way gives about {{ 1 / (1 - m.params.uplift_direct) - 1 | pct }}, since a flow that has lost {{ m.params.uplift_direct | pct }} gains that much when it comes back. This is near the {{ m.params.uplift_direct_high | pct }} of the Paris bypass line, whose services carried that much more than they would have without it (derived), new stations and time savings included.[[bypass]] The high case uses the bypass figure: {{ m.connecting.high.new | 1 }} million journeys. The low case drops the British figure and keeps the time model alone: {{ m.connecting.model_only.new | 1 }} million. Two differences remain: the British routes had their change inside one station, where Paris has a change of station, and French high-speed trips are much longer.

The market the survey does not see. The {{ m.conn.central | 1 }} million are people who already cross Paris by train. The bypass line shows a wider market: its trains round Paris carried 21.9 million passengers in 2009, up from 10.2 million in 1999,[[interco_sud]] and they are slow for the pairs that include the Atlantic line, 4h47 from Bordeaux to Lille against 4h21 with a change in Paris[[bxlille]] and about 3h25 through Paris by this study's arithmetic (to verify) assumption. Passengers who go by car or by air to avoid the change are not counted either. None of this is in the estimate: study point I13.

Growth and supply: the estimate has none

  • The Atlantic line is short of seats. A new operator, Velvet, has ordered 12 trains to run between Paris and Bordeaux, Nantes, Rennes and Angers from 2028, and says it will add 10 million seats a year; by its own account 15% of would-be rail passengers go by car, coach or air for want of trains.[[velvet]] The line to Lyon is being upgraded from 13 to 16 trains an hour each way by 2030.[[lgvse]]
  • The central case holds traffic at today's level for the whole life of the project. The growth case lets it rise {{ fp.growth | pct1 }} a year: {{ f.growth.tgv.paris | 0 }} million Paris-region TGV journeys a year instead of {{ f.central.tgv.paris | 0 }} million, {{ f.growth.tgv.new_before_charge | 1 }} million added by the project instead of {{ f.central.tgv.new_before_charge | 1 }}, and benefits worth {{ f.growth.econ_cover_all | pct }} of the yearly cost instead of {{ f.central.econ_cover_all | pct }} (see the economic return). Traffic growth is study point I17.

Capacity: what through platforms allow

A terminal platform holds a train for 30 to 40 minutes while it is emptied, cleaned and refilled, once the margin kept behind it is counted (derived from a 20-minute planning figure and a 26-minute quickest turn-round);[[rffcdg, ouigo]] a through platform holds it for {{ m.stop_min | 0 }}. Gare du Nord was turned down as the terminal of the airport express for lack of capacity.[[cdgx]] The new stations are sized for {{ m.extras.south_growth | pct }} more trains from the south than the standard hour assumed in this study (see how many platforms), and each of the six platform tracks at a station is loaded at {{ m.service_full.lyon_load | 1 }} trains an hour at most against the {{ m.per_track | 1 }} the rule allows. Whether the platforms or the lines outside Paris cap the number of trains today is study point I1.

If the platforms are the cap, each 10% of extra high-speed traffic would be {{ m.extras.capacity_journeys | 0 }} million journeys a year, about {{ m.extras.capacity_journeys / m.yearly.tgv_journeys | 0 }} times the gain counted in this chapter, and about €{{ m.extras.capacity_revenue * b.track_share | m }} million a year of track charges for the railway. Whether it comes depends on the lines outside Paris and on demand, so it stays out of the central case; the sensitivity test with 10% more traffic is in what could change the answer.

The financing of the project looks to those same passengers. The passenger charge tested there is €{{ fp.charge_end | 2 }} on each high-speed departure or arrival in Paris and €{{ fp.charge_through | 2 }} for a passenger who crosses the city on a direct train, on an average fare of €{{ b.fare | 2 }}. One way to lose fewer: collect the charge from the operators as a station charge per train, as track charges are collected today, rather than printing it on the ticket; how much of it the operators would pass on to fares is then their choice, and the loss of journeys is at most what is counted here.

What this means

The case for the project cannot rest on new high-speed traffic. It rests on the time saved by people who already travel, worth about €{{ m.yearly.tgv_benefit | m }} million a year to high-speed passengers and €{{ m.yearly.reg_benefit | m }} million to suburban and regional ones, on the parks that replace the surface tracks and, outside this estimate, on room for more trains later. The economic return adds these up, and what could change the answer tests each assumption of this chapter.
