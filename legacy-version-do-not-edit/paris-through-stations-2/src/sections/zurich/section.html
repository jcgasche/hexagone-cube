Zürich's main station was a terminus: 16 tracks ending at buffer stops in the middle of the city. Rather than enlarge it, the canton twice built a four-track through station underneath, in 1990 and in 2014, each with its own tunnel. The eight through tracks are under a third of the station's platform tracks and carry, on a rough count, close to half of its trains.

Four stations in one

Zürich Hauptbahnhof is four stations on one site: the surface hall, two underground through stations on its north and south edges, and a small terminus for two local lines under the forecourt. A level of passages and shops ties them together. The S-Bahn, named throughout, is the suburban rail network of the Zürich region. Parts of the station

The hall

  • What: 16 dead-end tracks on the surface, numbered 3 to 18, under a train shed of 1933, with seven island platforms and two side platforms.[[zh_wikihb_de]] Track 3 is the southern edge and track 18 the northern edge.[[zh_wikihb_2020]]
  • Platforms: lengthened to 420 m in 2002 to 2004.[[zh_tec21]]
  • Trains: long-distance trains that end here or turn back, international trains and four S-Bahn lines.[[zh_wikihb_en]] About 18 of the 27 long-distance departures in one hour of 2026 (derived).[[zh_board_dep]]
  • Before 1989: the tracks were numbered 1 to 16.[[zh_wikihb_de]]

Passages and shops

  • What: four passages run north to south under the hall tracks. Three of them form one complex with the shops under the forecourt.[[zh_wikihb_en]] Its two halls lie 4.7 m and 7.3 m below ground.[[zh_wikihb_de]]
  • Dates: first shops in 1970, enlarged in 1990 and 2014.[[zh_wikihb_de]] The westernmost passage was widened from about 10 m to about 35 m in 2005 to 2008, with a lift and escalators to each platform, for CHF 56.8 million.[[zh_geo158, zh_rr2007]]
  • Why it matters: the passages join both underground stations to each other and to the hall.[[zvv]] The station's standard minimum time for a change is 7 minutes.[[ch_probahn]]

The river Sihl

  • What: the river crosses the station under the tracks in five culverts about 190 m long, each about 12 m wide and 3 m high. The bridges that carry the 16 tracks are the roof of the culverts.[[zh_vaw]]
  • Under it: the roof of Löwenstrasse station lies about 1 m below the river bed,[[zh_vaw]] and its tracks 11 m below the river.[[zh_geo158]]
  • During the works: two of the five culverts were dried at a time, in four stages from April 2008 to December 2010, while the river ran in the other three. The site was to be cleared and flooded if a large flood was forecast.[[zh_geo158]]

Museumstrasse station

  • What: four through tracks, 41 to 44, on two island platforms 360 m long, 13.6 m below the street of the same name on the north side.[[zh_wikihb_de]]
  • Opened: May 1989 for a few regional trains,[[zh_wikihb_de]] and 27 May 1990 for the S-Bahn.[[zh_zvvhist]]
  • Trains: S-Bahn only, 11 lines.[[zh_wikihb_en]] Up to 20 trains an hour each way at the peak.[[sma]] Stadelhofen, the next station on the same lines, sees 770 trains a day.[[zh_stadelhofen]]
  • Cost: part of the line of 1990, CHF 866 million in all.[[zh_canton2001]]

Tunnels to the east

  • What: the Hirschengraben tunnel, 2.1 km underground of which 1.3 km bored, leads to Stadelhofen station; the Zürichberg tunnel, 5.0 km, carries on to the suburbs.[[zh_wikihg, zh_wikizb]] About 12 km of new line in all.[[sma]]
  • Opened: 27 May 1990, after nine years of works.[[zh_zvvhist]]
  • Cost: CHF 866 million with Museumstrasse station, of which the canton paid 80%.[[zh_canton2001]]
  • Today's limit: Stadelhofen has three tracks and is described as the largest bottleneck of the S-Bahn. A fourth track with second tunnel tubes is planned for 2027 to 2037 at CHF 1.1 billion.[[zh_stadelhofen]]

SZU terminus

  • What: two dead-end tracks, 21 and 22, with one island platform 125 m long, 11.6 m below the forecourt.[[zh_wikihb_de]] SZU is a separate local railway.
  • Opened: 5 May 1990. It uses a section built ahead for an underground railway that voters rejected in 1973.[[zh_wikiszu, zh_wikiubahn]]
  • Cost: a credit of CHF 72.4 million, approved by 67% of voters in 1983.[[zh_wikiubahn]]
  • Trains: two local lines, to the Sihl valley and to the Uetliberg, about seven trains an hour.[[zh_wikiszu]]

Sihlpost wing station

  • What: four extra dead-end tracks, 51 to 54, on the surface south of track 3 and set back from the hall. In service from 2003, closed on 15 June 2014 and removed.[[zh_wikihb_2020]]
  • Trains: the S-Bahn lines S1, S2, S8 and S14, which turned back here.[[zh_rr2000]]
  • Why: it freed hall tracks for the national timetable of 2004. The government accepted it only as an unavoidable stopgap: it met broad opposition because of longer changes and worse access to the city centre.[[zh_rr2000]]

Löwenstrasse station

  • What: four through tracks, 31 to 34, on two island platforms 420 m long and 13.5 m wide, 16 m below hall tracks 4 to 9.[[zvv, zh_tec21]] The whole structure is 675 m long.[[zvv]]
  • Opened: 15 June 2014 for the S-Bahn, 13 December 2015 for long-distance trains.[[zh_rrb946, zh_srf2015]]
  • Cost: CHF 674 million for the station.[[zh]]
  • Trains: 460 a day, of which 140 long-distance and 320 S-Bahn.[[zvv]] In 2026: four Intercity lines between western and eastern Switzerland, one regional express and the trains to Munich.[[zh_board_dep]]
  • Built under the working hall: its roof and walls were laid in 2002 to 2004, when the platforms above were being rebuilt anyway.[[zh_geo158]] See the lessons.

Weinberg tunnel

  • What: 4.8 km, two tracks in one tube more than 11 m across, from the east end of Löwenstrasse station to Oerlikon.[[zvv]] It passes under the river Limmat and 5 m below the Hirschengraben tunnel, then turns through about 180 degrees to reach Oerlikon from the south.[[zh_wikiweinberg]]
  • Built: main drive from June 2010, breakthrough on 22 November 2010.[[zh_sbbslides]] Opened with the station on 15 June 2014.[[zh_rrb946]]
  • Cost: CHF 498 million.[[zh]]
  • Trains: 12 S-Bahn trains an hour each way at opening.[[zh_rrb946]] Main station to airport in 9 minutes by Intercity.[[zh_search_air]]

Bridges to the west

  • What: two bridges, 1,156 m and 394 m long, carry the new line in from Altstetten, to the west.[[zvv]] A ramp leads down to the station; the approaches slope at up to 37 mm per metre.[[zh_sbbslides]]
  • Opened: December 2015. Long-distance trains have run through the station since then.[[zvv, zh_srf2015]]
  • The whole line: 9.6 km from Altstetten to Oerlikon.[[zh_rg2014]]

Why through platforms, and not a bigger terminus

Twice the first plan was something else, and twice voters chose a through station by a large majority.

YearWhat happened

1981They approve CHF 523 million for the S-Bahn alone, with a through station under the north edge of the main station: 74% yes.[[zh_wikiubahn, zh_canton2001]]

The vote shares of 1973 and 1981 and the dates of the wing station are from Wikipedia and unverified; the canton confirms the date and the amount of the vote of 1981.

The reasons given at the time were not journey times first.

  1. The terminus could not grow where it stood. The government found the scope to develop the station at its site and level "severely limited". A new main station on another site was examined and dropped.[[zh_rr2000]]
  2. The bottleneck was the line out, not only the platforms. Towards Oerlikon and the airport, more trains were "hardly possible". A through station joined to a new pair of tracks was called "the key element".[[zh_rr2000]]
  3. The wing station put passengers too far from their connections. The initiative called the walk from there to the express trains unacceptable; a through station under the hall gives "the most direct connections to the other tracks".[[zh_rr2000]]
  4. Turning back wastes platforms and tracks. The trade press called it "a time waster" and an "inefficient use of platform and track space".[[zh_rtcap]] The canton listed the end of the reversal among the gains.[[zh_rrb814]]
  5. It was built for long trains from the start. The canton judged that the S-Bahn alone would never fill a second through station. Platforms of 420 m instead of 300 m cost just under CHF 30 million more.[[zh_rr2000]]

The result, as stated by the engineers: four through tracks raised the capacity of the whole station by about one third.[[zh_tec21]] The gain was not published in trains per hour.

How to read it. A through train that waits for connections still stands 6 to 9 minutes. Only a train that does not wait shows the bare stop of 3 minutes, and a train that turns back can leave after 5. So the length of the stop is set mainly by the connections, not by the reversal (derived). What the through station removes is the reversal itself, the crossing of other trains' paths on the way in and out, and several minutes of approach. Where a locomotive must be changed at a reversal, that alone takes 4.5 minutes.[[ch_probahn]]

The benchmark for Paris. The four through tracks of Löwenstrasse take about 460 trains a day,[[zvv]] about 6 per track an hour on average over a service day of 19 to 20 hours (derived). The rule used for the Paris stations sizes a high-speed track at about {{ m.per_track | 1 }} trains an hour: a stop of {{ m.stop_min | 0 }} minutes, {{ m.clear_min | 0 }} minutes between a departure and the next arrival, and a timetable that uses {{ m.util | pct }} of that cycle assumption. With the lines full, the six tracks provided at each station are loaded at {{ m.service_full.nordest_load | 1 }} trains an hour per track at Nord-Est, {{ m.service_full.montparnasse_load | 1 }} at Montparnasse and {{ m.service_full.lyon_load | 1 }} at Gare de Lyon: see how many platforms. The two counts are not quite alike. Zürich mixes long-distance and suburban trains on the same four tracks, and its trains bunch around the hour; Paris gives regional express trains their own platforms and spreads the stops through the hour.

Traffic. The first through station changed the S-Bahn most. On a working day, 159,288 passengers crossed the city boundary by S-Bahn in the year before it opened, 196,854 in the first year after, and 445,562 in November 2014: 180% more in 24 years, with twice as many trains an hour at the peak.[[sma]] From 2014 to 2019, with the second through station, S-Bahn traffic grew by a further 19%.[[zvv19]] How much of this growth is due to the stations themselves cannot be separated from the growth of the region and of the service.

Trains. The station handled about 1,400 trains a day around 2000 and about 2,000 in 2007.[[zh_rt, zh_rg2007]] Around 2020 it handled 2,915 train movements a day, of which 2,607 passenger trains (unverified).[[zh_wikihb_2020]]

Journeys. S-Bahn trains cross the city about 6 minutes faster and long-distance trains up to 7 minutes faster; the main station is 9 minutes from the airport.[[zh_rrb946, zh_srf2013, zh_search_air]] A few journeys became longer.

Cost. The line of 1990 cost CHF 866 million in the final account. The canton paid CHF 693 million, 80% of it: CHF 14 million less than its credit of CHF 523 million once CHF 69 million of approved extra works and CHF 114 million of inflation were added.[[zh_canton2001]] The cross-city line of 2014 and 2015 was put to voters at CHF 1,450 million at 2000 prices.[[zh_init2001]] Its final account is CHF 2,052 million at 2005 prices, 1% above the ceiling of CHF 2,031 million agreed in 2008; the canton paid one third and the Confederation two thirds.[[zh]] In this study's 2026 euros the line comes to about €{{ [x for x in m.backtest if 'Zurich' in x.name][0].actual | 2 }} billion.

Punctuality: no figure is published for Zürich before and after the through stations. The regional transport authority states that the main station now has enough capacity for the growth expected in the foreseeable future and that timetable stability improved across Switzerland.[[zvv]]

What Paris can take from it, and where the comparison stops

What carries over

  • Put the through tracks where the trains already are. Both new stations lie under the old hall or right beside it, and one level of passages serves all of them. The wing station beside the hall was accepted only as a stopgap, because of the walk.
  • A few through tracks do a lot. Four of them added about a third to the capacity of the whole station.[[zh_tec21]]
  • Build for long trains at once. Platforms of 420 m were a small extra cost, and they are why Intercity trains use the station today.
  • It can be built under a working station. The roof slab, walls and columns went in first, from the surface; the station was then dug out under the slab. Normally two hall tracks at a time (two to three in the engineering press) were shortened by 100 m, leaving about 300 m of platform in use, and trains kept running on all the other tracks.[[zh_bartec, zh_tec21]] The listed south wing of the station building was underpinned and settled by 10 to 15 mm, against 25 mm allowed.[[zh_fib]] For Paris, see building methods.
  • The cost held once the works were let, not before. The second project ended 1% above the ceiling agreed in 2008, but that ceiling was 40% above the estimate put to voters in 2001, in prices five years older.[[zh, zh_init2001]]

Where the comparison stops

  • Size. Each Zürich through station has 4 tracks. Paris Nord-Est would have {{ m.cost.tracks['Paris Nord-Est'] | 0 }}, of which {{ m.cost.detail['Paris Nord-Est'].hs | 0 }} for high-speed trains, {{ m.cost.detail['Paris Nord-Est'].ter | 0 }} for regional express trains and {{ m.cost.detail['Paris Nord-Est'].sub | 0 }} for suburban trains; the three Paris stations together would have {{ sum(m.cost.tracks.values()) | 0 }}. Zürich is no evidence for a station of that size.
  • The hall stayed. About two thirds of Zürich's long-distance trains still end or turn back in the hall (derived). The Paris project moves all main-line trains underground and keeps no surface hall for trains. Zürich shows that through platforms relieve a terminus, not that they can replace one.
  • Trains and length. The platforms compare: 420 m in Zürich, 400 m for a double high-speed train in Paris.[[tsi]] The traffic does not: 140 long-distance trains a day use Löwenstrasse, about nine departures an hour. The pattern tested for Paris has {{ m.service.through_each_way | 0 }} high-speed trains an hour each way running through Nord-Est and {{ m.service.link_each_way | 0 }} more each way on the direct link between Montparnasse and Gare de Lyon: see the hour.
  • Depth and price. Löwenstrasse lies 16 m down and was dug from above. In 2026 euros it cost about €{{ [x for x in d.cost.stations if 'Zürich' in x.name][0].value | 2 }} million per metre of platform track (€{{ [x for x in d.cost.stations if 'Zürich' in x.name][0].lo | 1 }} to {{ [x for x in d.cost.stations if 'Zürich' in x.name][0].hi | 1 }} million), and that is a final cost. This study uses €{{ m.rates.platform_m * 1000 | 1 }} million for the deeper Paris stations, a planning price with {{ m.rates.risk | pct }} risk added on top: see the prices.
  • Stops. Running through did not shorten the stop of trains that wait for connections. A train that turns back in Zürich stands 5 to 12 minutes. In Paris no train is planned to reverse: every high-speed train calls at two of the three stations and runs on. The one exception: when the lines are full, {{ m.service_full.spare | 0 }} train an hour from the south-east is left without a partner and turns back at Nord-Est: see the direct link.
  • Time. Nine years from vote to opening for the first station, and fourteen for the second, from the vote of 2001 to the long-distance trains of December 2015.
The reading

Zürich supports three claims of this study: through tracks under a terminus add much capacity for their number, they can be built while the station works, and voters will pay for them when the alternative is a worse station further away. It does not support the size, the depth or the cost of the Paris stations, and its long-distance trains gained less time at the platform than one might expect.
